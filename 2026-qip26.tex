\begin{abstract}
In the projection lattice of a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, an
orthogonal basis---a \emph{context}---is characterised by the property
that its atomic probabilities sum to one on every state.  We study a
weakening of this condition in which two tuples of atoms induce the same
probability sum on every state.  Such pairs are called
\emph{pseudocontexts}.  In finite dimension this is equivalent to two
distinct rank-one decompositions of the same positive operator, placing
the notion naturally among finite frame-theoretic constructions.  Size
$k=1$ is trivial; size $k=2$ already admits genuinely nonorthogonal
examples over~$\mathbb{C}$; and, in fact, examples exist for every
$k\ge 2$.  For $k\ge 3$, genuine pseudocontexts also exist over
$\mathbb{R}$.  We present the operator formulation, give a general
explicit construction, analyse the spectral structure of the associated
structure operator, illustrate the symmetric $k=3$ case with a~figure,
and list the remaining classification problems.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{projection lattice, pseudocontext, rank-one decomposition,
structure operator, quantum contextuality}

\section{Introduction}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

Let $L$ be the lattice of projections in a %complex
Hilbert space of
finite dimension $n\ge 2$.  Its atoms (minimal non-zero elements)
correspond to one-dimensional subspaces, represented by nonzero
vectors.  States are described by probability measures
$P\colon L\to[0,1]$.  We denote by $S(L)$ the set of all states
on~$L$.

Vectors $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ form a \emph{context} if they compose an
orthogonal basis.  In this case (and only in this case) they satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \forall P\in S(L):\quad \sum_{i=1}^{n}P(a_i)=1.
  \label{eq:context}
\end{equation}
The present note concerns weaker additive identifications obtained by
comparing two tuples of atoms that are not required to be orthogonal.
Such \emph{pseudocontexts} are quantum-logical shadows of
orthomodular-lattice constructions of Mayet--Navara--Rogalewicz
\cite{Mayet_2000} and have direct physical meaning: they encode
state-independent empirical equivalences between families of generally
noncommuting observables.  It is then natural to ask for which sizes
$k$ and over which scalar fields genuine pseudocontexts exist.  The
answer is simple: pseudocontexts already exist for $k=2$ over
$\mathbb{C}$, and, more generally, for every $k\ge 2$; for $k\ge 3$
they exist over $\mathbb{R}$ as well.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Preliminaries}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

Throughout, $H$ is a %complex
Hilbert space of finite dimension
$n\ge 2$, and $L=L(H)$ is the orthomodular lattice of its closed
subspaces.  Its atoms correspond to one-dimensional subspaces, or
equivalently to \emph{rays} spanned by nonzero vectors.  We identify
an atom with any representative unit vector $a$ and write
$P_a=|a\rangle\langle a|$ for the orthogonal projection onto the ray
it spans.  More generally, for nonzero $a$ we set $P_a=|a\rangle\langle
a|/\langle a,a\rangle$, so that $P_a$ depends only on the ray $\mathbb
C a$.

A \emph{state} is a map $P\colon L\to[0,1]$ with $P(I)=1$ that is
finitely additive on orthogonal families.  In dimension $n\ge 3$,
Gleason's theorem \cite{Gleason} asserts that every state arises from
a density operator $\rho$ via
\begin{equation}
  P(a)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho P_a),\qquad
  \rho\ge 0,\quad\operatorname{tr}\rho=1.
  \label{eq:gleason}
\end{equation}
For $n=2$, Gleason's theorem fails for the abstract definition of a
state, and we adopt~\eqref{eq:gleason} as the definition: a state is
the assignment $P_a\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(\rho P_a)$ for some density
operator $\rho$.  Under either reading, the key consequence we use
repeatedly is that two self-adjoint operators that agree on every
state are equal as operators (the density operators in finite
dimension separate self-adjoint operators).



% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Contexts and pseudocontexts}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The operator form of \eqref{eq:context} is elementary and is the
starting point for everything that follows.

\begin{proposition}[Contexts]\label{prop:context}
Unit vectors $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in H$ form an orthonormal basis if and
only if
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{a_i}=I.
  \label{eq:context-op}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The forward implication is immediate.  For the converse, fix $j$ and
evaluate \eqref{eq:context-op} on the vector $a_j$:
\[
  1=\langle a_j,I\,a_j\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\langle a_i,a_j\rangle|^2.
\]
The term with $i=j$ already contributes $1$, so all others must vanish.
Hence $\langle a_i,a_j\rangle=0$ for $i\neq j$.  As this holds for
every $j$, the family is orthonormal, and being of size $n$ in an
$n$-dimensional space it is an orthonormal basis.
\end{proof}

Combined with \eqref{eq:gleason}, Proposition~\ref{prop:context}
recovers \eqref{eq:context} as a state-theoretic characterisation of
orthogonal bases.

\begin{definition}[Pseudocontext]\label{def:pc}
Let $k\ge 1$.  A \emph{pseudocontext of size $k$} is a pair of
$k$-tuples of atoms $(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$, $(b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)]  each tuple contains pairwise distinct atoms (i.e.\ no
              two of the $a_i$ are scalar multiples, and likewise for
              the $b_i$),
  \item[(ii)] the two tuples are disjoint as sets of rays, so that
              $|\{a_1,\ldots,a_k,b_1,\ldots,b_k\}|=2k$, and
  \item[(iii)] $\displaystyle\forall P\in S(L):\quad
               \sum_{i=1}^{k}P(a_i)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(b_i)$.
\end{enumerate}
A pseudocontext is called \emph{genuine} if neither $k$-tuple is a
context.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Condition~(i) excludes repeated atoms within a single tuple, and
condition~(ii) ensures the two tuples are truly distinct as collections
of rays. Without condition~(ii), every pseudocontext of size $k$
would yield pseudocontexts of every size $k'>k$ by adjoining $k'-k$
shared atoms; this trivial inflation can be carried out within the
original ambient subspace.
% , since $T+P_w=T+P_w$ for any unit
% vector~$w$, regardless of dimension.
Condition~(ii) rules out such
padding and makes the size $k$ a genuine invariant of the construction.
 % Both conditions are part of the definition; without them the
% notion degenerates trivially.
\end{remark}

By \eqref{eq:gleason}, condition~(iii) of Definition~\ref{def:pc} is
equivalent to the operator identity
\begin{equation}
  T:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{a_i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{b_i},
  \label{eq:opid}
\end{equation}
which we call the \emph{structure operator} of the pseudocontext.
Thus pseudocontexts are precisely pairs of distinct unit-norm rank-one
decompositions of a single positive operator.  This places them
naturally among finite frame-theoretic constructions: contexts are the
special case in which the structure operator is the identity on the
relevant subspace; in general, pseudocontexts are unit-norm rank-one
frame decompositions of a common positive operator.  If the common
operator is proportional to the identity on its support, the family
is a tight frame; after normalisation on that support one obtains a
POVM\@.

\begin{remark}[Connection to SIC-POVMs]
When the common structure operator satisfies $T=\frac{k}{n}I$ (tight
frame on all of $H$), each family $\{P_{a_1}, \ldots, P_{a_k}\}$ and $\{P_{b_1}, \ldots, P_{b_k}\}$
scaled by $n/k$ forms a symmetric informationally complete POVM
(SIC-POVM) if $k=n^2$ and the frame vectors are equiangular
\cite{renes2004}.  Pseudocontexts therefore generalise the SIC-POVM
notion by dropping both the tightness and equiangularity requirements.
\end{remark}

\section{The smallest cases}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{No pseudocontexts of size \texorpdfstring{$k=1$}{k=1}}

\begin{proposition}[Size $k=1$]\label{prop:k1}
There is no pseudocontext of size $k=1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The condition $P(a_1)=P(b_1)$ for all $P\in S(L)$ means that the two
self-adjoint operators $P_{a_1}$ and $P_{b_1}$ have equal expectation
in every density operator.  In finite dimension, density operators
separate self-adjoint operators, so $P_{a_1}=P_{b_1}$.  This rank-one
identity forces $a_1$ and $b_1$ to span the same ray, contradicting
the distinctness condition~(ii) of Definition~\ref{def:pc}.
\end{proof}


% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Pseudocontexts of size \texorpdfstring{$k=2$}{k=2} and the Bloch representation}
\label{sec:d2}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The $k=2$ case requires \textit{complex} scalars; the proof that no genuine
\textit{real} pseudocontext of size $k=2$ exists is given as
Theorem~\ref{thm:d2_real_complex}(ii) below.

In dimension two the pseudocontext condition admits a~complete
characterisation in terms of Bloch vectors.  This yields a unified and
basis-independent description of all pseudocontexts and clarifies the
real--complex dichotomy.

Every rank-one projector on $\mathbb{C}^2$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
  P_a=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\vec{r}_a\cdot\vec{\sigma}\bigr),
  \qquad \vec{r}_a\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \|\vec{r}_a\|=1,
  \label{eq:bloch}
\end{equation}
where $\vec{\sigma}=(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z)$ are the Pauli
matrices.  To be specific, for any unit vector $a=\alpha e_1+\beta e_2
\in\mathbb{C}^2$ in a chosen orthonormal basis $(e_1,e_2)$, the
components of the associated Bloch vector $\vec{r}_a=(r_x,r_y,r_z)$
are obtained by computing the expectation values of the Pauli
matrices:
\begin{align}
  r_x &= \langle a,\sigma_x a\rangle = 2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta), \notag \\
  r_y &= \langle a,\sigma_y a\rangle = 2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta),
        \label{eq:bloch_components} \\
  r_z &= \langle a,\sigma_z a\rangle = |\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2. \notag
\end{align}

\begin{proposition}[Bloch representation of pseudocontexts in dimension $d=2$]
\label{prop:bloch_pc}
Let $(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$ and $(b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ be $k$-tuples of unit
vectors in $\mathbb{C}^2$ with Bloch vectors
$\vec{r}_{a_i},\vec{r}_{b_i}\in\mathbb{R}^3$.  Then the following are
equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$ and $(b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ form a pseudocontext;
    \item $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{a_i}
            = \sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{b_i}$;
    \item $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\vec{r}_{a_i}
            = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\vec{r}_{b_i}$.
\end{enumerate}
The common structure operator $T$ has Bloch form
\[
  T=\tfrac{k}{2}I+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\vec{R}\cdot\vec{\sigma},
  \qquad \vec{R}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\vec{r}_{a_i}
              =\sum_{i=1}^{k}\vec{r}_{b_i}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Definition~\ref{def:pc} and~\eqref{eq:opid}, conditions~1 and~2 are
equivalent.  For the equivalence with~3, substitute the Bloch
representation~\eqref{eq:bloch} into the structure operator:
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{a_i}
  =\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(I+\vec{r}_{a_i}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\bigr)
  =\tfrac{k}{2}I+\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\vec{r}_{a_i}\Bigr)
   \cdot\vec{\sigma}.
\]
The identity component $\frac{k}{2}I$ is the same for both tuples, so
equality of the two structure operators reduces to equality of the
vector sums, giving the stated Bloch form of~$T$.
\end{proof}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Explicit \texorpdfstring{$k=2$}{k=2} example via Bloch vectors}
\label{sec:d2_example}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

We can now construct an explicit $k=2$ pseudocontext by directly
applying the Bloch representation and showing exactly how it arises
from non-orthogonal configurations.

\begin{proposition}[Existence of $k=2$ complex pseudocontexts]\label{prop:k2c}
There are genuinely nonorthogonal pseudocontexts of size ${k=2}$ in
$\mathbb{C}^n$ for every $n\ge 2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Embed $\mathbb{C}^2$ into $\mathbb{C}^n$ as the span of the first two
basis vectors $e_1,e_2$.  Define two pairs of unit vectors:
\[
  u_\pm=\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\,\pm\tfrac{1}{2},\,0,\ldots,0\Bigr),
  \qquad
  v_\pm=\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\,\pm\tfrac{i}{2},\,0,\ldots,0\Bigr).
\]
We obtain their Bloch vectors explicitly using
\eqref{eq:bloch_components}.  For $u_\pm$, setting
$\alpha=\sqrt{3}/2$ and $\beta=\pm 1/2$, we compute:
\begin{align*}
  r_x &= 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot
         (\pm\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr) = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \\
  r_y &= 2\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot
         (\pm\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr) = 0, \\
  r_z &= \bigl|\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\bigr|^2
         -\bigl|\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr|^2
       = \tfrac{3}{4}-\tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}.
\end{align*}
Thus $\vec{r}_{u_\pm}=(\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},0,\frac{1}{2})$, which
lie in the $xz$-plane of the Bloch sphere.

For $v_\pm$, setting $\alpha=\sqrt{3}/2$ and $\beta=\pm i/2$, we
compute:
\begin{align*}
  r_x &= 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot
         (\mp\tfrac{i}{2})\bigr) = 0, \\
  r_y &= 2\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot
         (\mp\tfrac{i}{2})\bigr) = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \\
  r_z &= \bigl|\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\bigr|^2
         -\bigl|\pm\tfrac{i}{2}\bigr|^2
       = \tfrac{3}{4}-\tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}.
\end{align*}
Thus $\vec{r}_{v_\pm}=(0,\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\frac{1}{2})$, which
lie in the $yz$-plane.

Summing the Bloch vectors within each pair yields the same structure
vector:
\[
  \vec{R}=\vec{r}_{u_+}+\vec{r}_{u_-}
        =\vec{r}_{v_+}+\vec{r}_{v_-}=(0,0,1).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:bloch_pc}, this guarantees that the operator
sums match in the two-dimensional subspace.  Embedding back into
$\mathbb{C}^n$ gives, with $V:=\mathrm{span}\{e_1,e_2\}$,
\begin{align}
  &P_{u_+}+P_{u_-}=P_{v_+}+P_{v_-}
   =\tfrac{3}{2}P_{e_1}+\tfrac{1}{2}P_{e_2}\nonumber\\
  &\;=\bigl(I_V+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_z\bigr)\oplus 0_{n-2}
   =\begin{pmatrix}3/2&0\\0&1/2\end{pmatrix}\oplus 0_{n-2}.\nonumber
\end{align}
The four rays are distinct: $u_+$ and $u_-$ differ by a sign in the
real second coordinate; $v_+$ and $v_-$ differ by a sign in the
imaginary second coordinate; and $u_\pm$ versus $v_\pm$ differ by a
factor of $i$ in the second coordinate, which is not a real scalar.
Within each pair, $\langle u_+,u_-\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\neq 0$ and
$\langle v_+,v_-\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\neq 0$, so neither pair is
orthogonal.  The pseudocontext is therefore genuine.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Bloch geometry of the complex $k=2$ example]
This construction directly illustrates the continuous $U(1)$ freedom
present in complex spaces.  The pair of Bloch vectors
$\{\vec{r}_{v_+},\vec{r}_{v_-}\}$ is related to
$\{\vec{r}_{u_+},\vec{r}_{u_-}\}$ by a $90^\circ$ rotation about the
$z$-axis (which aligns with their common sum~$\vec{R}$).  Because this
rotation preserves $\vec{R}$, it produces a distinct, valid
decomposition of the same fixed structure operator~$T$.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Classification in $\mathbb{C}^2$}

Pseudocontexts in dimension two correspond exactly to distinct
decompositions of a fixed vector $\vec{R}\in\mathbb{R}^3$ into $k$
unit vectors.

\begin{theorem}[Complete characterisation in $\mathbb{C}^2$]
\label{thm:d2_complex}
Let $k\ge 1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] For $k=1$, no pseudocontext exists.
\item[(ii)] For $k=2$, pseudocontexts exist and form continuous families.
\item[(iii)] For all $k\ge 3$, pseudocontexts exist; if $\vec{R}=0$,
they arise from zero-centroid configurations (e.g.\ regular polygons).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Part (i) is Proposition~\ref{prop:k1}.

For (ii), fix $\vec{R}\in\mathbb{R}^3$ with $0<\|\vec{R}\|<2$.  The
set of pairs $\{\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2\}$ of unit vectors satisfying
$\vec{r}_1+\vec{r}_2=\vec{R}$ is parametrised by a circle: writing
$\vec{r}_1=\vec{R}/2+\vec{w}$ and $\vec{r}_2=\vec{R}/2-\vec{w}$, the
unit-norm condition $\|\vec{r}_i\|=1$ becomes $\|\vec{w}\|^2
=1-\|\vec{R}\|^2/4$ together with $\vec{w}\perp\vec{R}$, so $\vec{w}$
ranges over a circle of radius $\sqrt{1-\|\vec{R}\|^2/4}$ in the plane
perpendicular to $\vec{R}$ through the origin.  Distinct positions
$\vec{w}\neq\pm\vec{w}'$ on this circle yield distinct unordered
pairs $\{\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2\}\neq\{\vec{r}_1^{\,\prime},\vec{r}_2^{\,\prime}\}$, and
hence (by Proposition~\ref{prop:bloch_pc}) two-tuples that form a
pseudocontext.

For (iii), take $\vec{R}=0$.  In $\mathbb{R}^3$ there are infinitely
many distinct triples of unit vectors with zero sum, e.g.\ the vertices
of any regular triangle centred at the origin, or any rotated version
thereof.  By Proposition~\ref{prop:bloch_pc}, any two such distinct
triples yield a pseudocontext of size $k=3$.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Real--complex dichotomy in two dimensions}

\begin{theorem}[Real vs.\ complex in $d=2$]
\label{thm:d2_real_complex}
$\;$
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] In $\mathbb{C}^2$, genuine pseudocontexts of size $k=2$
exist.
\item[(ii)] In $\mathbb{R}^2$, no genuine pseudocontext of size $k=2$
exists.
\item[(iii)] In $\mathbb{R}^2$, genuine pseudocontexts exist for all
$k\ge 3$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In $\mathbb{C}^2$, rotations about a fixed Bloch vector
$\vec{R}\neq 0$ form a continuous $U(1)$ symmetry, yielding
inequivalent decompositions; this is the content of
Theorem~\ref{thm:d2_complex}(ii).

In $\mathbb{R}^2$, Bloch vectors lie on a circle.  For $k=2$, the
condition $\vec{r}_1+\vec{r}_2=\vec{R}$ uniquely determines the pair
up to permutation, so no second decomposition exists.  If
$\vec{R}=0$, the vectors are orthogonal and form a context.

For $k\ge 3$, distinct planar configurations with identical vector
sum exist (e.g.\ rotated regular polygons).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Earlier observation]\label{rem:realvscomplex}
The nonexistence of genuine real pseudocontexts of size $k=2$ in
dimension two was observed in~\cite{NSvozil:QS24} without noticing
that the situation is different in complex spaces.  The Bloch picture
above makes the real--complex dichotomy transparent: over $\mathbb{R}$
a decomposition of a fixed structure operator into two rank-one terms
is unique up to permutation, whereas over $\mathbb{C}$ a continuous
phase degree of freedom produces inequivalent decompositions.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Spectral form}

\begin{proposition}[Spectrum in $d=2$]
\label{prop:d2_spectrum}
The eigenvalues of $T$ are
\[
  \lambda_{\pm}=\tfrac{k}{2}\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\|\vec{R}\|.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
The vector $\vec{R}$ completely parametrises pseudocontexts in
dimension two.
\end{remark}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Pseudocontexts of every size
         \texorpdfstring{$k\ge 2$}{k>=2}}
\label{sec:allk}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The $k=2$ case is best understood via the Bloch-sphere parametrisation;
the general $k$-construction below is its root-of-unity extension.  The
construction below uses a continuous phase $e^{i\phi}$ and is therefore
intrinsically complex; the corresponding existence statement over
$\mathbb{R}$ for $k\ge 3$ is covered separately by Example~\ref{ex:mb}
in Section~\ref{sec:symk3}.

\begin{theorem}[Complex-phase construction]\label{thm:allk}
Let $H$ contain an orthonormal pair $e_1,e_2$.  Fix an integer
$k\ge 2$, let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/k}$, choose $\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)$, and
choose $\phi\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $e^{i\phi}$ is not a $k$th root
of unity.  Define unit vectors
\begin{align*}
  a_j &= \cos\alpha\,e_1+\sin\alpha\,\omega^j e_2,\\
  b_j &= \cos\alpha\,e_1+\sin\alpha\,e^{i\phi}\omega^j e_2,
  \qquad j=0,\ldots,k-1.
\end{align*}
Then the $2k$ rays spanned by the $a_j$ and $b_j$ are pairwise
distinct, and
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}P_{a_j}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}P_{b_j}
  =k\bigl(\cos^2\!\alpha\;P_{e_1}+\sin^2\!\alpha\;P_{e_2}\bigr).
\]
Hence $(a_0,\ldots,a_{k-1})$ and $(b_0,\ldots,b_{k-1})$ form a
pseudocontext of size $k$.  If $\alpha\neq\pi/4$, then the
pseudocontext is genuine.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $c=\cos\alpha$ and $s=\sin\alpha$.  For each $j$,
\begin{align*}
  P_{a_j} &= c^2 P_{e_1}+s^2 P_{e_2}
             +cs\bigl(\omega^{-j}|e_1\rangle\langle e_2|
                     +\omega^{j}|e_2\rangle\langle e_1|\bigr),\\
  P_{b_j} &= c^2 P_{e_1}+s^2 P_{e_2}
             +cs\bigl(e^{-i\phi}\omega^{-j}|e_1\rangle\langle e_2|
                     +e^{i\phi}\omega^{j}|e_2\rangle\langle e_1|\bigr).
\end{align*}
Summing over $j=0,\ldots,k-1$ and using $\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\omega^j=0$
cancels all off-diagonal terms, giving
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}P_{a_j}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}P_{b_j}
  =k\bigl(c^2 P_{e_1}+s^2 P_{e_2}\bigr).
\]

\textit{Distinctness.}
If $a_j=\lambda a_\ell$ for some scalar $\lambda$, then comparing
first coordinates (which equal $c\neq 0$) gives $\lambda=1$, and then
$\omega^j=\omega^\ell$, so $j=\ell$.  Hence the $a_j$ are pairwise
distinct rays; the identical argument applies to the $b_j$.  If
$a_j=\lambda b_\ell$, then $\lambda=1$ and
$\omega^j=e^{i\phi}\omega^\ell$, i.e.\ $e^{i\phi}=\omega^{j-\ell}$,
contradicting the assumption that $e^{i\phi}$ is not a $k$th root of
unity.  Hence no $a_j$ coincides with any $b_\ell$.

\textit{Genuineness.}
For $j\neq\ell$ within the $a$-tuple,
\[
  \langle a_j,a_\ell\rangle = c^2+s^2\omega^{\ell-j}.
\]
This vanishes only if $c^2=s^2$ (i.e.\ $\alpha=\pi/4$) and
$\omega^{\ell-j}=-1$.  So for $\alpha\neq\pi/4$, no two distinct
vectors in the $a$-tuple are orthogonal.  An identical computation
gives $\langle b_j,b_\ell\rangle=c^2+s^2\omega^{\ell-j}$, so the same
conclusion holds for the $b$-tuple.  Hence neither tuple is a context
when $\alpha\neq\pi/4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:tight}
At the special value $\alpha=\pi/4$, the structure operator becomes
$\frac{k}{2}I_{\mathrm{span}\{e_1,e_2\}}$, so the construction yields
a unit-norm tight frame on the two-dimensional support.  For $k=2$
this reduces to a context; for $k\ge 3$ it gives a highly symmetric
pseudocontext (cf.\ Remark~\ref{rem:trine}).  In dimension $d=2$,
this construction reduces to the Bloch-vector description of
Sec.~\ref{sec:d2}.
\end{remark}

\section{A symmetric \texorpdfstring{$k=3$}{k=3} example}
\label{sec:symk3}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The case $k=3$ admits a very transparent planar representation; it is
the finite-dimensional analogue of the Mercedes--Benz frame.

\begin{example}[Mercedes--Benz type pseudocontext]\label{ex:mb}
Fix a two-dimensional subspace $V\subset H$ with orthonormal basis
$(e_1,e_2)$, and fix an angle $\theta$ satisfying
$\theta\not\equiv 0$, $\theta\not\equiv\pi/3$, and
$\theta\not\equiv 2\pi/3$ modulo~$\pi$.
Put, for $j=0,1,2$,
\[
  a_j=\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi j}{3}\Bigr)e_1
     +\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi j}{3}\Bigr)e_2,
\]
\[
  b_j=\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi j}{3}+\theta\Bigr)e_1
     +\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi j}{3}+\theta\Bigr)e_2.
\]
Then the six rays are pairwise distinct, and
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^{2}P_{a_j}=\sum_{j=0}^{2}P_{b_j}=\tfrac{3}{2}I_V.
\]
Thus $(a_0,a_1,a_2)$ and $(b_0,b_1,b_2)$ form a genuine
pseudocontext of size $k=3$ over $\mathbb{R}$.
\end{example}

\begin{proof}
In the basis $(e_1,e_2)$, each projector has the form
\[
  P_{\cos\varphi\,e_1+\sin\varphi\,e_2}
  =\begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\!\varphi&\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\\
                  \cos\varphi\sin\varphi&\sin^2\!\varphi\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For $\varphi_j=2\pi j/3$ and for $\varphi_j=\theta+2\pi j/3$, the
trigonometric identities
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^{2}\cos(2\varphi_j)=0,\qquad
  \sum_{j=0}^{2}\sin(2\varphi_j)=0,
\]
which hold for any arithmetic progression of three angles spaced
$2\pi/3$ apart, imply that the off-diagonal terms cancel while the
diagonal terms each sum to $3/2$.  Hence both sums equal
$\frac{3}{2}I_V$.

The three excluded residue classes modulo~$\pi$ account for all
collisions between the two triples:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\theta\equiv 0\pmod{\pi}$: every $b_j$ coincides with $a_j$
        or $-a_j$, giving the same ray.
  \item $\theta\equiv\pi/3\pmod{\pi}$: the $b$-triple is a cyclic
        permutation of the $a$-triple as a set of rays.
  \item $\theta\equiv 2\pi/3\pmod{\pi}$: the $b$-triple is again a
        permutation of the $a$-triple.
\end{itemize}
For all other values of $\theta$, the six rays are pairwise distinct.
Genuineness holds because for generic $\theta$ no two vectors within
the same triple are orthogonal (the angle between consecutive vectors
in each triple is $2\pi/3\neq\pi/2$), so neither triple is a context.
\end{proof}

\section{Spectral structure and canonical forms}
\label{sec:spectral}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

In dimension $d=2$, all spectral properties reduce to the Bloch form
of Sec.~\ref{sec:d2}.  We now analyse the general properties of the
structure operator $T=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{a_i}$ associated with a
$k$-tuple of unit vectors $(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$ in $H$.

\subsection{Gram representation and spectral equivalence}

\begin{definition}[Analysis operator and Gram matrix]
Let $A\colon\mathbb{C}^k\to H$ be defined by $Ae_i=a_i$, where $(e_i)$
is the standard basis of $\mathbb{C}^k$.  The \emph{analysis operator}
is $A^\dagger\colon H\to\mathbb{C}^k$, and the \emph{Gram matrix} is
\[
  G:=A^\dagger A=\bigl(\langle a_i,a_j\rangle\bigr)_{i,j=1}^k.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Frame operator factorisation]\label{prop:AAstar}
The structure operator admits the factorisation $T=AA^\dagger$, and
$G=A^\dagger A$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Spectral equivalence]\label{prop:spectral_equivalence}
The nonzero spectra of $T$ and $G$ coincide (including
multiplicities):
\[
  \sigma(T)\setminus\{0\}=\sigma(G)\setminus\{0\}.
\]
In particular, $\mathrm{rank}(T)=\mathrm{rank}(G)\le\min(k,n)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This is the standard singular-value correspondence between
$AA^\dagger$ and $A^\dagger A$: if $AA^\dagger v=\lambda v$ with
$\lambda\neq 0$, then $A^\dagger A(A^\dagger v)=\lambda(A^\dagger v)$
and $A^\dagger v\neq 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Spectral constraints}

\begin{proposition}[Trace identities]\label{prop:trace}
The structure operator satisfies
\[
  \operatorname{tr}(T)=k,\qquad
  \operatorname{tr}(T^2)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}|\langle a_i,a_j\rangle|^2.
\]
The diagonal terms $i=j$ each contribute $1$, giving
$\operatorname{tr}(T^2)\ge k$, with equality if and only if the
vectors are mutually orthogonal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\operatorname{tr}(T)=\sum_i\operatorname{tr}(P_{a_i})=k$.  For the
second identity,
$\operatorname{tr}(T^2)=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{tr}(P_{a_i}P_{a_j})
=\sum_{i,j}|\langle a_i,a_j\rangle|^2$.  Diagonal terms contribute~$k$;
off-diagonal terms are non-negative, and vanish simultaneously
if and only if all off-diagonal inner products are zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Non-orthogonality and spectral spread]
\label{prop:strict}
If the vectors $(a_i)$ are not mutually orthogonal, then
$\operatorname{tr}(T^2)>k$, the spectrum of $T$ is non-flat, and
$\lambda_{\max}(T)>1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Majorisation constraint]\label{prop:maj}
Suppose $k\le n$.  Let $\lambda(T)=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ be
the eigenvalues of $T$ in decreasing order (padded with zeros to
length $n$), and let $d=(1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^n$ have
$k$ ones and $n-k$ zeros.  Then $\lambda(T)\succ d$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The diagonal entries of $G$ are $G_{ii}=\|a_i\|^2=1$, so the diagonal
of $G$ is $(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^k$.  By the Schur--Horn theorem,
$\lambda(G)\succ(1,\ldots,1)$.  Since $G$ and $T$ share the same
nonzero eigenvalues
(Proposition~\ref{prop:spectral_equivalence}), padding $\lambda(G)$
with $n-k$ zeros gives $\lambda(T)$, and $\lambda(T)\succ d$ follows.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Overcomplete case $k>n$.}  When $k>n$ the
Gram matrix $G$ is $k\times k$ with rank at most $n$; the
majorisation is then best stated for the $k$-dimensional eigenvalue
vector of $G$ against $(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^k$, and the
Schur--Horn argument applies to $G$ directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Operator bounds]\label{prop:bounds}
The structure operator satisfies $0\le T\le kI$.  Moreover, if
$\mu:=\max_{i\neq j}|\langle a_i,a_j\rangle|$, then
\[
  \lambda_{\max}(T)\le 1+(k-1)\mu.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Positivity $T\ge 0$ is immediate, and $T\le kI$ since each
$P_{a_i}\le I$.  For the eigenvalue bound, apply the Ger\v{s}gorin
circle theorem to $G$: since $G_{ii}=1$ and $|G_{ij}|\le\mu$ for
$i\neq j$, every eigenvalue of $G$ lies in a disk centred at $1$ with
radius at most $(k-1)\mu$.  Since the nonzero eigenvalues of $T$ and
$G$ coincide, $\lambda_{\max}(T)\le 1+(k-1)\mu$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Commutant and symmetry}

\begin{definition}[Commutant]
The \emph{unitary commutant} of $T$ is
$\mathcal{C}(T):=\{U\in U(H):UT=TU\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Structure of the commutant]\label{prop:commutant}
Let $T=\sum_\alpha\lambda_\alpha P_\alpha$ be the spectral
decomposition of $T$ with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_\alpha$ and
orthogonal projections $P_\alpha$ of ranks $m_\alpha$.  Then
$\mathcal{C}(T)\cong\prod_\alpha U(m_\alpha)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
Spectral degeneracies ($m_\alpha>1$) give rise to continuous families
of distinct rank-one decompositions of $T$ (as in tight frames),
whereas a simple spectrum leads to strong rigidity.  This explains why
the tight-frame case (Remark~\ref{rem:tight}) is so rich in
pseudocontexts.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Canonical normal form}

\begin{proposition}[Spectral normal form]\label{prop:normalform}
Every structure operator is unitarily equivalent to
$\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r,0,\ldots,0)$ with
$\lambda_i>0$, $\sum_{i=1}^r\lambda_i=k$, and
$r=\mathrm{rank}(T)$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Canonical parametrisation of all decompositions}

\begin{theorem}[Canonical decomposition]\label{thm:canonical}
Let $T$ be a positive operator of rank $r$ with spectral decomposition
$T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$, $\lambda_i>0$, and
set $\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$.  Assume
$k\ge r$ (this is necessary, since $\mathrm{rank}(CC^\dagger)\le k$).

Every rank-one decomposition $T=\sum_{j=1}^{k}P_{a_j}$ with
$\|a_j\|=1$ corresponds to a matrix $C\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times k}$
satisfying
\begin{equation}
  CC^\dagger=\Lambda,\qquad \|c_j\|^2=1\;\text{ for all }j,
  \label{eq:Ccond}
\end{equation}
where $c_j$ is the $j$-th column of $C$, via
$a_j=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{ij}e_i$.  Conversely, every such $C$ defines a
valid decomposition.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $T=AA^\dagger$ with columns $a_j$.  In the eigenbasis of $T$,
write $A=UC$ where $U$ is the unitary embedding the eigenbasis of $T$
on its support into $H$.  Then
$CC^\dagger=U^\dagger TU\big|_{\mathrm{supp}(T)}=\Lambda$, and
$\|c_j\|=\|a_j\|=1$.  The converse is a direct computation.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Unitary freedom]\label{cor:unitary}
Among all rank-one decompositions $T=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|a_j\rangle\langle
a_j|$ into $k$ \emph{not necessarily} unit-norm vectors, any two are
related by $A\mapsto AU$ for some $U\in U(k)$ (this is the standard
$U(k)$-orbit on the affine fibre $\{A:AA^\dagger=T\}$).  The
unit-norm constraint $\|c_j\|=1$ in~\eqref{eq:Ccond} cuts out a
real-codimension-$k$ subvariety of this orbit, and what is relevant
for pseudocontexts is the quotient by phases acting on individual
columns and by permutations of columns, since rays---not vectors---are
the data.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Characterisation of pseudocontexts}

\begin{proposition}[Operator equivalence criterion]\label{prop:pseudo_equiv}
Two $k$-tuples $(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$ and $(b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ of distinct rays form
a~pseudocontext if and only if their matrices $C_A,C_B$ in the
eigenbasis of $T$ both satisfy~\eqref{eq:Ccond}:
\[
  C_A C_A^\dagger=C_B C_B^\dagger=\Lambda.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
Pseudocontexts correspond to distinct points in the space
$\{C\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times k}:CC^\dagger=\Lambda,\,\|c_j\|=1\}/{\sim}$,
where $\sim$ identifies decompositions that yield the same set of
rays.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Extremal spectral bounds}

\begin{proposition}[Rayleigh bounds]\label{prop:rayleigh}
For any density operator~$\rho$,
\[
  \lambda_{\min}(T)\le\operatorname{tr}(\rho T)\le\lambda_{\max}(T).
\]
Both bounds are tight, achieved by pure states on the corresponding
eigenvectors of $T$.
\end{proposition}

\section{Topological Forcing vs. Vectorial Representation: The Problem of ``Loose Ends''}
\label{sec:loose_ends}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The discrepancy between the purely geometric validity of the $k=2$ complex pseudocontext and the failure of its orthogonality hypergraph to enforce it highlights a crucial distinction in how pseudocontexts can arise.

One and the same configuration of observables---drawn as a Greechie-type orthogonality hypergraph---can admit very different types of probability assignments. These include classical probabilities (mediated by convex sums of two-valued $\{0,1\}$-states) as well as quantum probabilities (mediated by density operators via the Cauchy--Gleason theorem~\cite{Wright2019}). However, any valid probability assignment on the hypergraph, regardless of its origin, must satisfy the fundamental completeness condition: the sum of probabilities on any context must equal one (that is, $P(I) = 1$).

For certain tightly intertwined hypergraphs, this completeness condition alone is sufficient to structurally force a pseudocontext identity. The proof technique, successfully utilized in \cite{NSvozil:QS24} and applicable to configurations like the aforementioned octagon, relies on finding two distinct ``coverings.'' By summing the completeness conditions of a specific subset of contexts containing the first pseudocontext, and comparing it to a second subset containing the second pseudocontext, one can algebraically cancel out all intermediate auxiliary vertices. When this topological cancellation is perfect, the equality of the pseudocontext probability sums becomes a rigid, state-independent logical necessity of the hypergraph itself, applying equally to classical and quantum probabilities.

However, this covering technique cannot be applied to the $k=2$ complex pseudocontext formed by $u_\pm$ and $v_\pm$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:k2c}. If we attempt to equate the probability sums of the $u$-pair and the $v$-pair using the contexts provided, we obtain:
\begin{align}
    P(u_+) + P(u_-) &= 2 - 2P(e_3) - \bigl(P(w_+) + P(w_-)\bigr), \label{eq:u_sum} \\
    P(v_+) + P(v_-) &= 2 - 2P(e_3) - \bigl(P(z_+) + P(z_-)\bigr). \label{eq:v_sum}
\end{align}
Subtracting \eqref{eq:v_sum} from \eqref{eq:u_sum} yields:
\begin{align}
    &P(u_+)+P(u_-) - \bigl(P(v_+)+P(v_-)\bigr) \nonumber \\
    &= P(z_+)+P(z_-) - \bigl(P(w_+)+P(w_-)\bigr).
\end{align}
For the pseudocontext identity to be forced purely by the graph, the right-hand side would need to be forced to zero. But $w_\pm$ and $z_\pm$ are ``loose ends'': they each belong to exactly one context in the hypergraph. Because they do not intertwine with any other contexts, their probability values are completely unconstrained relative to one another. There is no topological mechanism to force $P(w_+)+P(w_-) = P(z_+)+P(z_-)$.

It is precisely these loose ends that allow the classical $\{0,1\}$-valuation (which assigns 0 to $w_\pm$ and 1 to $z_\pm$) to comfortably violate the pseudocontext equality. The identity $P(u_+)+P(u_-) = P(v_+)+P(v_-)$ holds for these specific quantum observables solely due to the ``miracle'' of their specific complex vector representation, not due to the logical skeleton of their orthogonalities.

To achieve a structurally forced pseudocontext---one where the identity is dictated purely by the graph---we require tighter bounds and configurations without such loose ends. The vertices must intertwine in such a way that they form closed topological loops, enabling the exact algebraic cancellations utilized in the covering proofs. This motivates the search for, and the study of, more complex, higher-dimensional hypergraphs where pseudocontextuality is embedded directly into the orthomodular logic.


\section{The Octagon Pseudocontext via Distinct Coverings}
\label{s:octagon}

We can rigorously demonstrate the pseudocontextual nature of a tightened---relative to the previous configuration involving $u_\pm$ and $v_\pm$---octagon hypergraph by employing a proof technique based on mutually distinct ``coverings,'' a method we previously utilized in \cite{NSvozil:QS24}.

The hypergraph consists of 20 vertices and 12 contexts of size three. We label the 16 outer vertices along the perimeter as follows: let $v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_7$ be the midpoints of the eight octagon edges, and let $v_{01}, v_{12}, \ldots, v_{70}$ be the eight corners. The perimeter is formed by eight straight-line contexts:
\begin{equation*}
    E_i = \{v_{i-1, i},\; v_i,\; v_{i, i+1}\} \quad \text{for } i=0,\dots,7 \pmod 8.
\end{equation*}
Notice that adjacent midpoints, such as $v_1$ and $v_2$, are not on the same context; rather, their respective perimeter contexts $E_1$ and $E_2$ intertwine at the shared corner vertex $v_{12}$.

The remaining four inner contexts connect opposite midpoints and pass through the four inner vertices $a_1, a_2$ and $b_1, b_2$:
\begin{align*}
    D_0 &= \{v_0, a_1, v_4\}, \quad D_2 = \{v_2, a_2, v_6\}, \\
    D_1 &= \{v_1, b_2, v_5\}, \quad D_3 = \{v_3, b_1, v_7\}.
\end{align*}

We aim to prove that the sums of probabilities on the inner pairs match: $P(a_1)+P(a_2) = P(b_1)+P(b_2)$. To do so, we construct two distinct coverings of the hypergraph.

\textbf{Covering A} focuses on the first pseudocontext pair $\{a_1, a_2\}$, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:octagon_coverings}(a). We construct it by taking the inner contexts $D_0, D_2$ along with four alternating perimeter edges:
\begin{equation*}
    A = \{D_0, D_2, E_1, E_3, E_5, E_7\}.
\end{equation*}
Summing the completeness condition over these six contexts yields a total probability of 6. Let us evaluate the multiplicity of each vertex covered by $A$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The inner vertices $a_1, a_2$ are covered exactly once.
    \item The midpoints $v_0, v_4$ and $v_2, v_6$ are covered exactly once (by $D_0, D_2$).
    \item The remaining midpoints $v_1, v_3, v_5, v_7$ are covered exactly once (by $E_1, E_3, E_5, E_7$).
    \item Each of the eight corners $v_{i, i+1}$ is covered exactly once because the selected alternating perimeter contexts perfectly span all corners without overlapping.
\end{itemize}
Thus, covering A perfectly partitions all 16 outer vertices, covering each exactly once:
\begin{equation}
    P(a_1) + P(a_2) + \sum_{i=0}^7 P(v_i) + \sum_{\text{corners}} P(v_{i, i+1}) = 6. \label{eq:covA}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Covering B} focuses on the second pseudocontext pair $\{b_1, b_2\}$, depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:octagon_coverings}(b). We construct it using the other two inner contexts $D_1, D_3$ alongside the complementary set of four alternating perimeter edges:
\begin{equation*}
    B = \{D_1, D_3, E_0, E_2, E_4, E_6\}.
\end{equation*}
Summing over these six contexts also yields 6. Evaluating the vertex multiplicities reveals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The inner vertices $b_1, b_2$ are covered exactly once.
    \item The midpoints $v_1, v_5$ and $v_3, v_7$ are covered exactly once (by $D_1, D_3$).
    \item The remaining midpoints $v_0, v_2, v_4, v_6$ are covered exactly once (by $E_0, E_2, E_4, E_6$).
    \item The selected perimeter contexts $E_0, E_2, E_4, E_6$ again perfectly span all eight corners exactly once.
\end{itemize}
Hence, Covering B covers the same set of 16 outer vertices, each exactly once:
\begin{equation}
    P(b_1) + P(b_2) + \sum_{i=0}^7 P(v_i) + \sum_{\text{corners}} P(v_{i, i+1}) = 6. \label{eq:covB}
\end{equation}

Because Covering A and Covering B perfectly match in their multiplicities over all 16 outer perimeter vertices, we can directly equate \eqref{eq:covA} and \eqref{eq:covB}. The perimeter terms completely cancel out, leaving:
\begin{equation*}
    P(a_1)+P(a_2) = P(b_1)+P(b_2).
\end{equation*}
This rigorously establishes the state-independent probabilistic equivalence between the two pairs.

% ================================================================
\subsection{The parameter \(C\)}
\label{app:C}
% ================================================================

In the octagon coordinatization---that is, the search for a faithful orthogonal representation~\cite{lovasz-79} of the uniform hypergraph~\cite{Bretto-MR3077516} in which vectors assigned to adjacent vertices (or, more generally, to vertices within each hyperedge) are required to be orthogonal---it is convenient to parametrize the inner pair by a single positive parameter \(C\).
Since only rays matter, all vectors below are understood up to a nonzero scalar multiple.

\begin{proposition}[Meaning of the parameter \(C\)]
Let \(s>0\), set \(C:=s^2\), and choose representatives
\[
  a_1 =  (s,1,0),\qquad a_2 =  (s,-1,0),
\]
with companion rays
\[
  b_1 =  (s,i,0),\qquad b_2 =  (s,-i,0).
\]
Then the inner pair \(\{a_1,a_2\}\) has mutual angle \(2\alpha\)
satisfying
\[
  \cos(2\alpha)=\frac{C-1}{C+1},
  \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
  C=\cot^2\alpha.
\]
In particular, \(C=1\) is the orthogonal case.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The normalized representatives of \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) are
\[
  \frac{(s,1,0)}{\sqrt{s^2+1}},\qquad
  \frac{(s,-1,0)}{\sqrt{s^2+1}}.
\]
Their inner product equals \((s^2-1)/(s^2+1)\), which gives the
stated formula after substituting \(C=s^2\).  The same computation
applies to the \(b\)-pair.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The point of the parameter \(C\) is that, in the explicit octagon
coordinatization used in the main text, the closure conditions depend
on the inner pair only through this single scalar.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Exact \(\mathbb C^3\) realization at \(C=1\)]
\label{ex:C1}
Let
\[
  p=\frac{\sqrt{14}+\sqrt{2}}{4},
  \qquad
  q=\frac{\sqrt{14}-\sqrt{2}}{4}.
\]
Then
\[
  p^2+q^2=2,
  \qquad
  pq=\frac34.
\]

A convenient choice of the inner rays intersection at the additional point $c=(0,0,1)$ is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  a_1 & =  (1,1,0), & a_2 & =  (1,-1,0),\\
  b_1 & =  (1,i,0), & b_2 & =  (1,-i,0).
\end{aligned}
\]

One compatible choice of the eight midpoint rays is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  v_0 & =  (1,-1,\sqrt2),       & v_4 & =  (1,-1,-\sqrt2),\\
  v_1 & =  (1,i,p+iq),          & v_5 & =  (1,i,-p-iq),\\
  v_2 & =  (1,1,-i\sqrt2),      & v_6 & =  (1,1,i\sqrt2),\\
  v_3 & =  (1,-i,q-ip),         & v_7 & =  (1,-i,-q+ip).
\end{aligned}
\]

One compatible choice of the eight corner rays is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  v_{01} & =  \bigl(-p+i(q+\sqrt2),\ \sqrt2-p+iq,\ 1-i\bigr),\\
  v_{12} & =  \bigl(\sqrt2-p+iq,\ p-i(q+\sqrt2),\ 1+i\bigr),\\
  v_{23} & =  \bigl(q+\sqrt2+ip,\ -q+i(\sqrt2-p),\ -1+i\bigr),\\
  v_{34} & =  \bigl(q+i(p-\sqrt2),\ q+\sqrt2+ip,\ -1-i\bigr),\\
  v_{45} & =  \bigl(p-i(q+\sqrt2),\ -\sqrt2+p-iq,\ 1-i\bigr),\\
  v_{56} & =  \bigl(-\sqrt2+p-iq,\ -p+i(q+\sqrt2),\ 1+i\bigr),\\
  v_{67} & =  \bigl(-q-\sqrt2-ip,\ q-i(\sqrt2-p),\ -1+i\bigr),\\
  v_{70} & =  \bigl(-q-i(p-\sqrt2),\ -q-\sqrt2-ip,\ -1-i\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]

A direct symbolic check shows that each listed context is orthogonal.
The identities \(p^2+q^2=2\) and \(pq=\tfrac34\) are sufficient for
the simplification.  Hence the octagon from Section~\ref{s:octagon}
admits an exact \(\mathbb C^3\) realization at \(C=1\).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The coordinates above are not unique.  Any common unitary
transformation, together with arbitrary rescalings of the individual
ray representatives, yields an equivalent realization.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Phase symmetry]
The exact \(\mathbb C^3\) realization exploits a complex phase
symmetry in the third coordinate.  This is one of the reasons why the
construction is much more rigid over \(\mathbb R\) than over
\(\mathbb C\).
\end{remark}





% ================================================================
\subsection{No faithful real realization at \(C=1\)}
\label{app:real}
% ================================================================

Example~\ref{ex:C1} provides an exact realization of the octagon hypergraph in \(\mathbb{C}^3\) at the orthogonal limit \(C=1\), where the inner pseudocontext splits into two contexts. We show that no faithful realization by real rays exists.

\begin{proposition}[No faithful real realization at \(C=1\)]
\label{prop:no_real_C1}
The octagon hypergraph of Section~\ref{s:octagon}, augmented by the two inner \(C=1\) contexts intersecting at the vertex \(c\), admits no faithful representation by rays in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume a faithful real realization exists. By a global orthogonal transformation, fix \(c=(0,0,1)^T\). All rays sharing a context with \(c\) lie in the \(xy\)-plane. Using rotational freedom in that plane, set
\[
  a_1=(1,0,0)^T,\qquad a_2=(0,1,0)^T.
\]
Let
\[
  b_1=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)^T,\qquad b_2=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)^T,
\]
with \(\theta\notin \frac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{Z}\) by faithfulness. Define \(m=\tan\theta\neq 0\) and \(M=1+m^2>1\).

Let the midpoint rays be \(v_j=(X_j,Y_j,Z_j)^T\).

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Nonplanarity of midpoint rays.}
Every midpoint lies in exactly one inner context, paired with one of
the planar reference vectors:
\(v_0,v_4\in D_0\) (with \(a_1\));
\(v_2,v_6\in D_2\) (with \(a_2\));
\(v_1,v_5\in D_1\) (with \(b_2\));
\(v_3,v_7\in D_3\) (with \(b_1\)).
Suppose \(Z_j=0\), so that \(v_j\) lies in the \(xy\)-plane.
If \(v_j\) is paired with \(a_1\) (resp.\ \(a_2\)), orthogonality forces
\(X_j=0\) (resp.\ \(Y_j=0\)), leaving \(v_j\) proportional to \(a_2\)
(resp.\ \(a_1\)). If \(v_j\) is paired with \(b_1\) or \(b_2\), the
relations \(Y=mX\) or \(X=-mY\) together with \(Z_j=0\) force
coincidence with one of the \(b\)-rays. In every case this violates
faithfulness.
Hence \(Z_j\neq 0\) for all \(j\), and we may normalize
\[
  v_j=(X_j,Y_j,1)^T.
\]

The inner contexts impose:
\begin{align*}
D_0:&\quad X_0=X_4=0,\qquad Y_0Y_4+1=0,\\
D_1:&\quad Y_1=mX_1,\quad Y_5=mX_5,\quad X_1X_5 M+1=0,\\
D_3:&\quad X_3=-mY_3,\quad X_7=-mY_7,\quad Y_3Y_7 M+1=0.
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
  X_5=-\frac{1}{M X_1},\qquad Y_7=-\frac{1}{M Y_3}.
\]
The quantities \(X_1\) and \(Y_3\) are nonzero: if \(X_1=0\) (resp.\ \(Y_3=0\)),
then \(v_1\) (resp.\ \(v_3\)) becomes collinear with a coordinate direction,
forcing vertex identifications incompatible with faithfulness.

For perimeter contexts, the condition that adjacent corner rays are orthogonal yields
\[
  (v_{j-1}\cdot v_j)(v_j\cdot v_{j+1})-(v_{j-1}\cdot v_{j+1})\|v_j\|^2=0.
\]
Evaluating at \(j=0\) and \(j=4\) gives
\begin{equation}
Y_0 = \frac{Y_7+mX_1}{1-mX_1Y_7}, \qquad
Y_4 = \frac{Y_3+mX_5}{1-mY_3X_5}, \label{eq:Y0a4tight}
\end{equation}
where the denominators are nonvanishing: if \(1-mX_1Y_7=0\), the
closure equation at \(j=0\) reduces to \(Y_7+mX_1=0\), and combining
this with \(mX_1Y_7=1\) yields \(-m^2X_1^2=1\), which has no real
solution. The case \(1-mY_3X_5=0\) at \(j=4\) is excluded analogously.

\medskip\noindent
Substituting the inner relations into \eqref{eq:Y0a4tight}  and simplifying the compound fractions yields
\[
  Y_0=\frac{m M X_1Y_3-1}{M Y_3+mX_1},\qquad
  Y_4=\frac{M X_1Y_3-m}{M X_1+mY_3}.
\]
Using \(Y_0Y_4=-1\) from \(D_0\), set \(u=X_1Y_3\). Expanding the product of these fractions, collecting terms in \(u\), and applying the identity \(M=1+m^2\) causes all mixed terms to cancel, yielding the master constraint (see Appendix~\ref{app:master_derivation})
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:master}
(M^2 u^2 + m u + 1) + M(X_1^2+Y_3^2)=0.
\end{equation}

Since \(X_1,Y_3\in\mathbb{R}\),
\[
  X_1^2+Y_3^2 \ge 2|X_1Y_3|=2|u|,
\]
and therefore the left-hand side is bounded below by
\[
  f(u)=M^2 u^2 + m u + 1 + 2M|u|.
\]
Using \(M=1+m^2\), one has \(2M-|m|>0\), hence
\[
  M^2 u^2 + m u + 2M|u| \ge M^2 u^2 + |u|(2M-|m|)\ge 0,
\]
with equality only at \(u=0\). Consequently \(f(u)\ge 1\) for all \(u\in\mathbb{R}\), contradicting the required vanishing. Therefore no real realization exists.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Minimality of the obstruction]
Of the fourteen orthogonality relations defining the \(C=1\) octagon,
the proof above invokes only the three inner contexts \(D_0\), \(D_1\),
\(D_3\) and the two perimeter closures at \(j=0\) and \(j=4\); the
remaining nine relations are not used. The impossibility therefore
holds already for this five-relation sub-pattern.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Complex realization]
In the complex construction, one has formally \(m=i\), hence \(M=1+i^2=0\). The relations above then become singular, and the coercive quadratic structure responsible for the contradiction disappears. Substituting \(M=0\) reduces the constraint to \(iu+1=0\), which admits solutions in \(\mathbb{C}\).
\end{remark}

\section{Physical interpretation and constraints}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

A pseudocontext $(a_1,\ldots,a_k)$, $(b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ implements a~state-independent
empirical equivalence between two families of rank-one observables
$A_i=P_{a_i}$ and $B_i=P_{b_i}$.  Although the operators within each
family are generally noncommuting, the two linear functionals
\[
  \rho\longmapsto\sum_i\operatorname{tr}(\rho A_i),\qquad
  \rho\longmapsto\sum_i\operatorname{tr}(\rho B_i)
\]
agree on the whole state space.

The spectral decomposition of $T$ establishes absolute quantum limits
on these probability sums.  By Proposition~\ref{prop:rayleigh},
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\min}(T)\le\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(a_i)\le\lambda_{\max}(T).
  \label{eq:qbounds}
\end{equation}
These bounds are tight.

For the 36-atom hypergraph example \cite{2023-navara-svozil}, the
structure operator $T_2=\mathrm{diag}(1/5,1/5,13/5)$ confines the
quantum probability sum to $[1/5,\,13/5]=[0.2,\,2.6]$.  A classical
hidden-variable model based on two-valued ($\{0,1\}$) truth
assignments to projections---subject to the consistency requirement
that exactly one atom in each orthogonal basis receives the
value~$1$---would allow the sum to range over $\{0,1,2,3\}$, which
includes the extreme values $0$ and $3$.  Because $T_2$ has no
eigenvalue equal to $0$ or $3$, quantum mechanics forbids these
extremes; the quantum system cannot reach them.  This discrepancy is
a signature of Kochen--Specker-type contextuality
\cite{cabello-96,kochen1}.

This state-independent additive equivalence is related to, but
distinct from, the operational notion of contextuality introduced by
Spekkens \cite{Spekkens-04}, which applies to arbitrary experimental
procedures (preparations, transformations, and unsharp measurements)
rather than to sharp projective measurements alone.  A precise
comparison is beyond the scope of this note.

In the language of quantum information, $(A_1,\ldots,A_k)$ and
$(B_1,\ldots,B_k)$ are distinct rank-one decompositions of the same
positive operator.  When this operator is proportional to the identity
on its support, the families form tight frames; after normalisation
they yield POVMs.

\section{Open problems}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{problem}[Classification]
Classify, up to unitary equivalence, all pseudocontexts of size $k$
in $\mathbb{C}^n$.  Even for small $(k,n)$, the orbit structure is
not yet transparent.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Lattice representability]
Characterise which orthomodular lattices carrying a
pseudocontext-type additive identification admit a faithful embedding
into some $L(\mathbb{C}^n)$, sharpening the partial results of
\cite{2023-navara-svozil,navara-2024-e}.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Frame-symmetry characterisation]
Can one characterise pseudocontexts purely in terms of symmetries of
the underlying rank-one frame, for example in terms of unitaries
commuting with the structure operator and permuting the atoms of the
two decompositions?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Higher-dimensional pseudocontexts]
Classify pseudocontexts that genuinely span a higher-dimensional subspace.
% , beyond the $k=3$ examples of \cite{2023-navara-svozil,navara-2024-e}.
% The constructions of Sec.~\ref{sec:allk} and the Mercedes--Benz
% example of Sec.~\ref{sec:symk3} live in two-dimensional subspaces
% (cf.\ Remark~\ref{rem:minimaldim}).  Of independent interest are
% pseudocontexts that genuinely span a~higher-dimensional subspace.
In particular, in
\cite{2023-navara-svozil,navara-2024-e} and here in Section~\ref{s:octagon}, we studied systems of vectors

Definition~\ref{def:pc} for any vector representation
fulfilling the orthogonality constraints.  The systematic
classification of such higher-dimensional pseudocontexts remains

\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Classification of minimal real pseudocontexts]
Theorem~\ref{thm:d2_real_complex} establishes that genuine real
pseudocontexts of size $k=2$ do not exist in any dimension, while
Example~\ref{ex:mb} demonstrates their existence for $k=3$.  Thus

all such minimal ($k=3$) examples up to orthogonal equivalence over
$\mathbb{R}$.
\end{problem}

\section{Derivation of the Master Constraint}
\label{app:master_derivation}

We provide a step-by-step verification of the algebraic derivation
leading from the perimeter closure conditions to the master
constraint~\eqref{eq:master} used in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:no_real_C1}.

The starting point is the perimeter closure condition stated in the
proof: for three rays $v_{j-1}, v_j, v_{j+1}$ forming a context,
adjacent corner rays are orthogonal, yielding
\begin{equation}
(v_{j-1}\cdot v_j)(v_j\cdot v_{j+1})-(v_{j-1}\cdot v_{j+1})\|v_j\|^2=0.
\label{eq:closure_condition}
\end{equation}
Evaluating~\eqref{eq:closure_condition} at $j=0$ and $j=4$ produces
the relations
\begin{align}
Y_0 &= \frac{Y_7+mX_1}{1-mX_1Y_7}, \label{eq:Y0_app} \\
Y_4 &= \frac{Y_3+mX_5}{1-mY_3X_5}, \label{eq:Y4_app}
\end{align}
as stated in the proof. The inner contexts impose the constraints
\begin{equation}
Y_7 = -\frac{1}{MY_3},\qquad
X_5 = -\frac{1}{MX_1},\qquad
M = 1+m^2,
\label{eq:inner_constraints_app}
\end{equation}
together with the requirement $Y_0Y_4 = -1$ from context $D_0$.

\subsection*{Simplifying the fractions}

Substituting $Y_7$ from~\eqref{eq:inner_constraints_app} into~\eqref{eq:Y0_app}:
\begin{align}
Y_0 &= \frac{-\frac{1}{MY_3}+mX_1}{1-mX_1\bigl(-\frac{1}{MY_3}\bigr)}
     %= \frac{\frac{-1+mMX_1Y_3}{MY_3}}{\frac{MY_3+mX_1}{MY_3}}
     = \frac{mMX_1Y_3-1}{MY_3+mX_1}. \label{eq:Y0_simplified}
\end{align}
Substituting $X_5$ from~\eqref{eq:inner_constraints_app} into~\eqref{eq:Y4_app}:
\begin{align}
Y_4 &= \frac{Y_3+m\bigl(-\frac{1}{MX_1}\bigr)}{1-mY_3\bigl(-\frac{1}{MX_1}\bigr)}
     %= \frac{\frac{MY_3X_1-m}{MX_1}}{\frac{MX_1+mY_3}{MX_1}}
     = \frac{MX_1Y_3-m}{MX_1+mY_3}. \label{eq:Y4_simplified}
\end{align}

\subsection*{Constructing the product condition}

Let $u = X_1Y_3$. Using the constraint $Y_0Y_4 = -1$ from context
$D_0$ together with~\eqref{eq:Y0_simplified} and~\eqref{eq:Y4_simplified}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{mMu-1}{mX_1+MY_3}\cdot\frac{Mu-m}{MX_1+mY_3} = -1.
\label{eq:product_condition}
\end{equation}
Cross-multiplying~\eqref{eq:product_condition} yields
\begin{equation}
(mMu-1)(Mu-m) = -(mX_1+MY_3)(MX_1+mY_3).
\label{eq:cross_multiplied}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Expanding both sides}

The left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:cross_multiplied} expands as
\begin{align}
\text{LHS} &= mM^2u^2 - m^2Mu - Mu + m \nonumber\\
           &= mM^2u^2 - Mu(m^2+1) + m. \label{eq:LHS_expanded}
\end{align}
Since $M = 1+m^2$, the coefficient $m^2+1$ equals $M$, giving
\begin{equation}
\text{LHS} = mM^2u^2 - M^2u + m.
\label{eq:LHS_final}
\end{equation}

The right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:cross_multiplied} expands as
\begin{align}
\text{RHS} &= -\bigl[mMX_1^2 + m^2X_1Y_3 + M^2X_1Y_3 + mMY_3^2\bigr] \nonumber\\
           &= -\bigl[u(M^2+m^2) + mM(X_1^2+Y_3^2)\bigr].
\label{eq:RHS_final}
\end{align}

\subsection*{Cancellation and the master constraint}

Equating~\eqref{eq:LHS_final} and~\eqref{eq:RHS_final}:
\begin{equation}
mM^2u^2 - M^2u + m = -u(M^2+m^2) - mM(X_1^2+Y_3^2).
\label{eq:equated}
\end{equation}
Adding $M^2u$ to both sides, the $-M^2u$ term cancels:
\begin{equation}
mM^2u^2 + m = -m^2u - mM(X_1^2+Y_3^2).
\label{eq:cancelled}
\end{equation}
Bringing all terms to the left and dividing by $m \neq 0$ (since
$\theta \notin \frac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{Z}$ by faithfulness) yields the
master constraint~\eqref{eq:master} in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:no_real_C1}.

\subsection*{Positivity contradiction}

Since $X_1,Y_3 \in \mathbb{R}$, the inequality
$X_1^2+Y_3^2 \geq 2|X_1Y_3| = 2|u|$ holds. The left-hand side
of~\eqref{eq:master} therefore satisfies
\begin{align}
&M^2u^2 + mu + 1 + M(X_1^2+Y_3^2) \nonumber\\
  &\geq M^2u^2 + mu + 1 + 2M|u| \nonumber\\
  &= M^2u^2 + |u|(2M - |m|) + 1.
\label{eq:bounded}
\end{align}
With $M = 1+m^2 > 1$, we have
$2M - |m| = 2+2m^2-|m| > 0$ for all real $m$. Consequently the
expression in~\eqref{eq:bounded} is strictly positive for every
$u \in \mathbb{R}$, contradicting~\eqref{eq:master}. This
completes the algebraic verification of the master constraint and
the resulting impossibility of a real realization.

Yes, the algebra is genuinely straightforward. A reviewer with a pen and a piece of scratch paper can verify it in about three minutes using high-school level algebra.

Here is exactly what the reviewer will write down to verify your claim.

**1. Simplifying the fractions:**
Substituting $Y_7 = \frac{-1}{M Y_3}$ into $Y_0$:
\[
Y_0 = \frac{\frac{-1}{MY_3} + mX_1}{1 - mX_1(\frac{-1}{MY_3})} = \frac{\frac{-1 + mMX_1Y_3}{MY_3}}{\frac{MY_3 + mX_1}{MY_3}} = \frac{mMX_1Y_3 - 1}{MY_3 + mX_1}
\]
Substituting $X_5 = \frac{-1}{M X_1}$ into $Y_4$:
\[
Y_4 = \frac{Y_3 + m(\frac{-1}{MX_1})}{1 - mY_3(\frac{-1}{MX_1})} = \frac{\frac{MY_3X_1 - m}{MX_1}}{\frac{MX_1 + mY_3}{MX_1}} = \frac{MX_1Y_3 - m}{MX_1 + mY_3}
\]

**2. Setting up $Y_0 Y_4 = -1$:**
Letting $u = X_1 Y_3$, the product $Y_0 Y_4 = -1$ becomes:
\[
\frac{m M u - 1}{m X_1 + M Y_3} \cdot \frac{M u - m}{M X_1 + m Y_3} = -1
\]
Cross-multiplying to clear the denominators:
\[
(m M u - 1)(M u - m) = - (m X_1 + M Y_3)(M X_1 + m Y_3)
\]

**3. Expanding the Left-Hand Side (LHS):**
\[
\text{LHS} = m M^2 u^2 - m^2 M u - M u + m
\]
Factor out $-Mu$ from the middle terms:
\[
\text{LHS} = m M^2 u^2 - M u (m^2 + 1) + m
\]
Since $M = 1 + m^2$, the term $(m^2+1)$ is just $M$. So:
\[
\text{LHS} = m M^2 u^2 - M^2 u + m
\]

**4. Expanding the Right-Hand Side (RHS):**
\[
\text{RHS} = -[m M X_1^2 + m^2 X_1 Y_3 + M^2 Y_3 X_1 + m M Y_3^2]
\]
Substitute $u = X_1 Y_3$ and group the $X_1^2$ and $Y_3^2$ terms:
\[
\text{RHS} = -[u(M^2 + m^2) + m M(X_1^2 + Y_3^2)]
\]

**5. Setting LHS = RHS and canceling:**
\[
m M^2 u^2 - M^2 u + m = - M^2 u - m^2 u - m M (X_1^2 + Y_3^2)
\]
Add $M^2 u$ to both sides. The $-M^2 u$ completely cancels out!
\[
m M^2 u^2 + m = - m^2 u - m M (X_1^2 + Y_3^2)
\]
Move everything to the left side:
\[
m M^2 u^2 + m^2 u + m + m M (X_1^2 + Y_3^2) = 0
\]
Finally, divide the entire equation by $m$ (which we know is non-zero):
\[
M^2 u^2 + m u + 1 + M (X_1^2 + Y_3^2) = 0
\]

### Conclusion
As you can see, there are no massive polynomials, no tricky substitutions, and no pages of factoring. The "miracle" of the terms canceling out ($M^2 u$) happens almost immediately upon applying $M = 1+m^2$.
