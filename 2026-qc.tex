\title{Partition-Resolved Coherence in Quantum Algorithms}

\author{Karl Svozil \orcidlink{0000-0001-6554-2802}}
\email{karl.svozil@tuwien.ac.at}
\affiliation{Institute for Theoretical Physics, TU Wien,
Wiedner Hauptstra{\ss}e 8--10/136, 1040 Vienna, Austria}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Quantum computation does not provide branchwise readout of many classical
evaluations.  Its operational resource is coherent processing that encodes
problem structure into amplitudes and resolves a selected property by
interference.  We formulate a decision problem as a partition of an instance
set.  Exact resolution is possible if and only if the encoded states associated with
different partition cells occupy orthogonal output subspaces; the readout may
then be represented either by a Hermitian observable whose spectral
projectors label the cells or by a unitary that writes the cell label into an
output register.  A separating family of such partitions can support complete
instance identification, although one partition measurement need not reveal
the complete instance.  As a concrete model, we introduce an inaccessible
witness into the Bernstein--Vazirani oracle.  Perfect witness records erase
selected interference coordinates, while nonorthogonal witness states give a
continuous visibility parameter and an explicit output distribution.  The
model separates global reversible evaluation from accessible, partition-resolving
recombination and clarifies the roles of oracle access, erasure, contextuality,
and uniformity.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{quantum computation, quantum interference, partition identification, oracle leakage, quantum speedup}

\section{Introduction}

A common description of quantum computation says that a quantum computer
evaluates a function on many inputs ``at once.''  The image is seductive: a
register is placed in a superposition, a function is applied to all
components, and a final measurement extracts the desired answer.  This
picture is often invoked when discussing Deutsch--Jozsa~\cite{deutsch:92},
Simon's algorithm~\cite{Simon1997}, Shor's algorithm~\cite{shor:94}, Grover
search~\cite{grover}, and related oracle or Fourier-sampling
procedures~\cite{cleve:98,nielsen-book}.

Taken literally, however, the picture is wrong.  A quantum computer does not
return a list of all evaluated values.  The components of a superposition are
not independently readable, copyable, or archivable classical records.  They
are amplitudes in one Hilbert-space state, and a run of the final measurement
returns one outcome label---possibly a multibit label---with probabilities
fixed by the Born rule.

The organizing thesis of this article is that many quantum decision and
identification tasks are naturally encoded by a partition \(\Pi\) of a
problem-instance set \(\Omega\).  The immediate task is to identify the cell
\([\omega]_\Pi\), rather than necessarily the complete instance \(\omega\).
A suitable coherent encoding and recombination can make different cells
orthogonal and hence exactly distinguishable.  Equivalently, the cell may be
resolved by a Hermitian operator whose eigenspaces represent the cells, or by
a unitary operator that transfers the cell label to a readout register.  A
family of partitions whose common refinement is the singleton partition can,
when the associated measurements are physically available, yield complete
instance identification.  This partition/operator formulation is developed
in Sec.~\ref{sec:partition-operators}.

This formulation does not make coarseness the source of quantum advantage.
The operative requirement is an end-to-end efficient match among state
preparation, oracle access, coherent transformation, and readout.  A measured
partition label may also be an intermediate invariant from which classical
post-processing recovers a complete answer, as in order finding and
factoring.  Conversely, an arbitrary partition of black-box data need not
admit an efficient quantum resolution.

The claim is not meant to settle interpretive questions about Everettian or
many-worlds accounts of quantum computation.  Advocates of such accounts have
argued that quantum algorithms can be understood in terms of computations
distributed across branches or worlds~\cite{deutsch,hewitt-horsman-2009,
wallace2012emergent}.  The narrower claim is operational and
model-theoretic: superposition components are not independently readable
classical records, whereas coherent global evolution can convert phase
relations into an accessible partition label or other task-relevant
statistic.

The technical contribution is the leakage construction in
Appendix~\ref{app:leaky-bv}.  It treats the accessible oracle as a channel
obtained from a globally unitary interaction with an inaccessible witness.
Besides the discrete case in which \(k\) path coordinates are recorded
perfectly, a continuous version assigns nonorthogonal witness states with
overlaps \(\lambda_i\).  This yields an explicit interpolation between exact
Bernstein--Vazirani interference and ordinary value-query behavior.  The
later sections separately delimit what follows from unitary reversibility,
Kochen--Specker contextuality, and computational uniformity.

\section{Superposition is not branchwise readout}

Let an $n$-qubit state be written in the computational basis as
\[
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\alpha_x\ket{x}.
\]
The dimension of the Hilbert space is $2^n$, and this exponential dimension is often used to motivate quantum parallelism. But the amplitudes $\alpha_x$ are not classical memory cells. A measurement yields one outcome per run, with probabilities determined by the Born rule. Recovering the amplitudes requires many similarly prepared systems and many measurement settings, that is, tomography.

Thus a superposition can be acted on linearly, but it cannot be read branch by branch. Holevo's theorem gives one precise expression of this limitation. For an ensemble of states on a Hilbert space $\mathcal H$, the accessible classical information is bounded above by the Holevo quantity $\chi$, and $\chi\leq \log_2 \dim \mathcal H$. Hence, for an unassisted ensemble encoded in an $n$-qubit system, the accessible classical information per use is at most $n$ bits~\cite{Holevo1973,nielsen-book}. (Entanglement-assisted communication is a different resource setting.)

The state vector may contain exponentially many amplitudes, but the accessible classical readout is sharply constrained. This is the first reason why the naive parallelism metaphor is misleading. The superposed components can interfere, but they are not simultaneously available classical facts.

\section{Partitions, complete identification, and resolving operators}
\label{sec:partition-operators}

Let \(\Omega\) be a finite set of problem instances.  A decision or
identification task is specified by a partition
\begin{equation}
        \Pi=\{C_1,\ldots,C_m\},
        \qquad
        \Omega=\biguplus_{j=1}^m C_j .
\end{equation}
Here every \(C_j\) is nonempty, the cells are pairwise disjoint, and their
union is \(\Omega\).  Equivalently, \(\Pi\) is the set of equivalence classes
of the relation
\(
\omega\sim_\Pi\omega'
\)
if and only if \(\omega\) and \(\omega'\) belong to the same cell \(C_j\).
For an unknown instance \(\omega\in\Omega\), the required answer is the cell
\([\omega]_\Pi=C_j\) containing it.  The task need not identify
\(\omega\) completely.  Conversely, a family of partitions
\(\Pi^{(1)},\ldots,\Pi^{(r)}\) is \emph{separating} when its common
refinement is the partition whose cells are all nonempty intersections
of one cell from each \(\Pi^{(\ell)}\).  We denote this common refinement
by \(\bigwedge_{\ell=1}^r\Pi^{(\ell)}\); it is the discrete partition when
\begin{equation}
        \bigwedge_{\ell=1}^r\Pi^{(\ell)}
        =
        \bigl\{\{\omega\}:\omega\in\Omega\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Resolving all members of a separating family therefore gives complete
instance identification.  Here ``complete identification'' refers to the
classical instance label; it is not tomography of an arbitrary unknown
quantum state.

Suppose that the preparation, oracle call, and coherent post-processing
associate an output state \(\rho_\omega\) on a Hilbert space
\(\mathcal H_{\rm out}\) with each instance.  The partition \(\Pi\) is
resolved exactly by projectors \(P_1,\ldots,P_m\) when
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
        P_jP_{j'}&=\delta_{jj'}P_j,
        \qquad \sum_{j=1}^mP_j=I,\\
        \operatorname{Tr}(P_j\rho_\omega)&=1
        \quad\text{for }\omega\in C_j .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:partition-projectors}
\end{equation}
Thus the supports of states belonging to different cells must occupy
orthogonal output subspaces.  States within one cell need not be mutually
distinguishable: their deliberately retained equivalence is precisely the
coarse-graining encoded by \(\Pi\).

For bounded-error algorithms, the exact projective condition is replaced
by a POVM \(\{M_j\}\) satisfying
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
        M_j&\succeq0,\qquad \sum_jM_j=I,\\
        \operatorname{Tr}(M_j\rho_\omega)&\geq1-\epsilon
        \quad(\omega\in C_j),\qquad \epsilon<\tfrac12 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Different cells then need not have orthogonal supports; the relevant
constraints are quantitative distinguishability bounds.  The exact
formulation above isolates the ideal case in which the desired partition
label is perfectly resolvable and is not intended as a characterization of
all bounded-error or sampling-based quantum algorithms.

Choose distinct real numbers \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\).  The Hermitian
operator
\begin{equation}
        A_\Pi=\sum_{j=1}^m\lambda_jP_j
\label{eq:partition-observable}
\end{equation}
then resolves the partition: its outcome \(\lambda_j\) identifies \(C_j\).
Conversely, the spectral projectors of any Hermitian operator that exactly
separates the cells supply projectors satisfying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:partition-projectors}.  A partition problem can therefore be
encoded into, and resolved by, the spectral structure of a suitable
observable.

There is an equivalent unitary form for pure encoded states.  If
\(\mathcal H_j=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{\psi_\omega}:\omega\in C_j\}\)
and the subspaces \(\mathcal H_j\) are mutually orthogonal, then an isometry,
and hence a unitary extension on a sufficiently large space, can be chosen
such that
\begin{equation}
        U_\Pi\ket{\psi_\omega}\ket{0}_L
        =
        \ket{\eta_\omega}\ket{j}_L,
        \qquad \omega\in C_j .
\label{eq:partition-unitary}
\end{equation}
Measuring the label register \(L\) resolves the same partition.  The
Hermitian and unitary descriptions are not competing mechanisms:
\(U_\Pi\) coherently transfers the cell information to a standard readout,
and \(A_\Pi\) describes the associated spectral measurement.  Equation
\eqref{eq:partition-unitary} gives the corresponding label-register
realization.

Complete identification by several partitions requires additional physical
conditions.  If the corresponding observables are jointly measurable, their
joint spectral refinement may be measured directly.  If they are
incompatible, resolving all partitions generally requires separately
prepared systems, sequential instruments with specified disturbance, or a
different unitary that resolves the singleton refinement in one enlarged
output space.  Partition separation is therefore a logical condition; the
existence and efficiency of the required preparation, oracle, unitary, and
measurement are the physical and computational conditions.

\section{The useful mechanism: structure-matched recombination}

Many oracle-based and Fourier-sampling quantum algorithms have the schematic form
\begin{align*}
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
&\xrightarrow{\text{spread}}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x\ket{x} \\
&\xrightarrow{\text{compute}}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x} \\
&\xrightarrow{\text{detect}}
\textnormal{measurable interference pattern}.
\end{align*}
The middle step may be produced by phase kickback. If
\[
U_f\ket{x}\ket{y}=\ket{x}\ket{y\oplus f(x)}
\]
and the target register  \(\ket{y}\) is prepared in
\(
\ket{-}=\frac{\ket{0}-\ket{1}}{\sqrt2},
\)
then
\[
U_f\ket{x}\ket{-}=(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x}\ket{-}.
\]
The values of $f(x)$ are not read out individually. They are converted into phases. The final measurement is useful only if those phases are recombined in a basis matched to the structure one wants to detect.

This is the mechanism sometimes summarized as spread, compute, detect, or
spread, encode, resolve.  The algorithm distributes amplitude, encodes
structure coherently, and recombines the amplitudes so that constructive and
destructive interference concentrate a relevant invariant in the spectral
subspaces of a chosen readout observable.

Here ``structure-matched'' is an operational criterion, not a claim that
every successful algorithm has been explained merely by renaming its output.
For a partition \(\Pi\), the match consists of efficiently preparable inputs
and oracle access, an efficiently implementable transformation that produces
the orthogonal cell subspaces of Eq.~\eqref{eq:partition-projectors}, and an
efficient readout of the corresponding observable.  In the examples below
the match takes two main forms: character, period, or hidden-subgroup
structure, as in Bernstein--Vazirani, Simon, and Shor, where a Fourier
transform turns an invariant into spectral concentration; and geometric
concentration in a low-dimensional invariant subspace, as in Grover search
and the Hein--Tanner lattice examples.

Deutsch--Jozsa is the simplest example. After phase kickback, the query register is in the state
\[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x}.
\]
Applying the final Hadamard transform $H^{\otimes n}$ gives the amplitude of $\ket{0^n}$ as
\[
\bra{0^n}H^{\otimes n}
\left(
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x}
\right)
=
\frac{1}{2^n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}.
\]
This amplitude is \(\pm1\) for constant functions and \(0\) for balanced
functions.  The promise makes the global predicate visible as complete
constructive or destructive interference.  The Deutsch--Jozsa promise
problem therefore uses one exact quantum query, whereas a deterministic
classical black-box algorithm requires \(2^{n-1}+1\) queries in the worst
case.  It is used here as a transparent interference example, not as a claim
of an exponential bounded-error separation: randomized classical sampling
distinguishes constant from balanced with \(O(1)\) queries at bounded error.

This example also clarifies what the one-query quantum result concerns. It is not the recovery of a unique classical table of values. It is an exact solution of a decision problem whose answer is a coarse-grained class, because that class is exactly the invariant concentrated by the final interference pattern.  For the original one-bit Deutsch problem, the four functions
\[
        f:\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}
\]
are not individually identified.  They are partitioned into the two classes
\[
        \Pi_D
        =
        \bigl\{\{00,11\},\{01,10\}\bigr\},
\]
where the strings denote the ordered value pairs \(f(0)f(1)\).  Equivalently, the accessible answer is the parity
\[
        f(0)\oplus f(1),
\]
or, in the usual terminology, the distinction between constant and balanced functions.  The final interference pattern is therefore not a readout of \(f(0)\) and \(f(1)\) separately.  It is a readout of the partition cell containing \(f\).

In the operator language of Sec.~\ref{sec:partition-operators}, the final
Hadamard transform maps the two cells of \(\Pi_D\) to orthogonal output
subspaces.  Measuring the associated two-eigenvalue Hermitian observable
resolves that partition, while a corresponding unitary may write the parity
label to a separate register.  Other partitions of the four truth tables can
be combined with \(\Pi_D\); if their common refinement is discrete, their
labels support complete truth-table identification, subject to the required
oracle calls and compatibility conditions.  Earlier work on quantum state
identification used precisely this language for generalized Deutsch-type
problems: trivalent functions of a trit were grouped into equipartitions, and
the question was whether the corresponding functional classes could be
separated by quantum state identifications~\cite{2007-tkadlec-svozil-springer}.

This is not a strict opposition between partial and full answers.
Coarse-graining is a common way for a task to align with interference, but it
is not the operative criterion.  The operative criterion is the efficient
physical realization of the partition projectors or their unitary label
map.  Arbitrary partitions of arbitrary black-box data need not have this
property, and a partition-resolving intermediate statistic may sometimes be
classically processed into a complete answer.

Shor's algorithm is a more sophisticated example. Modular exponentiation is computed reversibly, but the algorithm does not read all values of
\[
f(x)=a^x\bmod N.
\]
Instead, periodicity is sampled through a quantum Fourier transform. The success comes from algebraic structure: translation symmetry becomes spectral concentration.  Shor's algorithm is therefore an important warning against saying that interference is useful only for ``partial'' knowledge.  The quantum subroutine produces samples from which the order can be inferred; with the standard random choice of $a$, checks, and repetitions, continued-fraction and greatest-common-divisor post-processing yields a nontrivial factor of \(N\) with high probability. The final classical answer may be complete even when the quantum readout is an invariant.

The same observation explains why generic predicates are not made easy by
superposition.  Let a black-box string \(b=(b_1,\ldots,b_N)\) be accessed by
the value oracle
\[
        O_b\ket{i}\ket{y}
        =
        \ket{i}\ket{y\oplus b_i}.
\]
Its parity defines the two-cell partition
\[
\begin{split}
        \Pi_{\rm par}&=\{C_0,C_1\},\\
        C_p&=\left\{b\in\{0,1\}^N:
        \bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}b_i=p\right\}.
\end{split}
\]
Although \(\Pi_{\rm par}\) has only two cells, they are maximally interlaced
on the Boolean cube: flipping any one bit moves an instance from one cell to
the other.  Moreover, the projection of either cell onto any proper subset
of the coordinates is the full cube on that subset.  Thus every assignment
to fewer than \(N\) coordinates has completions in both cells; no such
partial coordinate information favors either parity label.

On the formal instance space spanned by the basis states \(\ket{b}\), the
corresponding partition observable may be written as
\[
        A_{\rm par}
        =
        \sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^N}
        (-1)^{\sum_i b_i}\ket{b}\!\bra{b}
        =
        Z_1Z_2\cdots Z_N.
\]
It is a weight-\(N\) observable: its eigenvalue is precisely the parity-cell
label.  This expression does not make the black-box problem trivial.  If an
accessible register already contained \(\ket{b}\), the observable could be
measured directly.  Query complexity instead asks how many uses of \(O_b\)
are required to encode this global label into distinguishable output
subspaces.  The partition must therefore be considered together with its
oracle representation, not merely by counting its cells.

The polynomial method makes this obstruction precise.  After \(T\) oracle
queries, every output amplitude is a multilinear polynomial in the input
bits of degree at most \(T\), and every measurement probability has degree
at most \(2T\).  Any probability that separates \(C_0\) from \(C_1\) with
bounded error must approximate the parity character
\[
        (-1)^{\sum_i b_i}
        =
        \prod_{i=1}^{N}(-1)^{b_i},
\]
whose approximate degree is \(N\).  Hence \(2T\geq N\).  This bound is
tight: pairing the input positions allows the parity of each pair to be
resolved with one quantum query, so exact parity requires
\(\lceil N/2\rceil\) queries.  The improvement over the deterministic
classical query complexity is therefore at most a factor of
two~\cite{Farhi-98,bbcmw-01}.

The original Deutsch problem discussed above is precisely the \(N=2\)
special case of this partition: \(\Pi_D=\Pi_{\rm par}\).  Its two truth-table
classes are already interlaced on the Boolean square, yet the bound
\(2T\geq N\) then requires only \(T\geq1\), which the one-query Deutsch
algorithm attains.  Interlacing is therefore compatible with a factor-of-two
quantum reduction; for unrestricted parity it obstructs any larger
asymptotic improvement~\cite{deutsch,Farhi-98}.

More generally, in the black-box query model, polynomial-method bounds show
that for total Boolean functions the bounded-error quantum and classical
query complexities are polynomially related, ruling out arbitrary
exponential speedup for such functions~\cite{bbcmw-01}.  Superposition alone
is therefore not the resource.  Structure-matched recombination is.

Appendix~\ref{app:leaky-bv} gives a concrete interpolation for the
Bernstein--Vazirani problem.  A globally unitary oracle couples a tunable part
of the computational-basis path label to an inaccessible witness.  The
Boolean function in the answer register is unchanged, but the oracle
available to the algorithm is a reduced channel whose interference pattern
is progressively suppressed.  The appendix also shows that interference and
full value recovery are composable: the surviving recombination recovers the
coordinates still represented by orthogonal partition subspaces, and
ordinary value queries can supply the rest.

Another illustration of this principle is provided by spatial-search and wave-communication constructions on regular lattices.  The basic image is that of a coherent wave spread over a whole lattice.  A locally marked site acts like a resonant defect: it is different enough from the background to be distinguished, but close enough in frequency to the extended lattice mode to exchange amplitude with it.  The lattice then functions as a global carrier.  In the lattice-search analysis of Hein and Tanner, this picture is made precise by an avoided crossing between the extended symmetric state and a localized defect state.  The resulting dynamics rotates amplitude from the delocalized state into the marked localization.  In their related wave-communication proposal, the same mechanism is extended to several marked vertices: with two marked defects, amplitude can be transferred between sender and receiver through the symmetric carrier state~\cite{hein2010quantum,hein2010wave}.

This is a spectral, spatial-search analogue of Grover-type amplitude
amplification: coherent wave interference converts a phase or scattering
perturbation into an observable amplitude concentration.  Grover search also
clarifies why ``structure'' should not be inflated until it explains
everything.  The database has no exploitable character, period, or
hidden-subgroup promise; the useful structure is the two-dimensional geometry
spanned by the uniform state and the marked subspace.  The corresponding
black-box improvement is quadratic and asymptotically optimal~\cite{grover,
benn:97}.

The same interference mechanism has classical coherent-wave analogues, but
that fact is not an efficient classical simulation of the encoded quantum
algorithm.  A wave realization may pay explicitly for the full lattice or
mode structure, whereas a quantum implementation may encode the walk space
more economically when the structure permits.  Interference is therefore the
positive mechanism, not by itself an account of computational advantage.  An
advantage requires end-to-end economy in preparation, oracle access,
encoding, transformation, and readout, typically under a promise or oracle
model.



\section{Uncomputation, information transfer, and reset}

A second misconception concerns garbage.  Closed-system evolution is
unitary,
\[
\ket{\psi}\mapsto U\ket{\psi},
\qquad
U^\dagger U=I.
\]
A non-injective classical function therefore cannot be implemented by a
unitary acting only on the input register.  It must be embedded reversibly,
for example as
\[
U_f:\ket{x}\ket{y}\mapsto\ket{x}\ket{y\oplus f(x)}.
\]
The information distinguishing the inputs remains somewhere in the global
state.

If a reversible computation produces
\[
\ket{x}_A\ket{0}_B\ket{0}_C
\longmapsto
\ket{x}_A\ket{f(x)}_B\ket{g(x)}_C,
\]
the garbage register \(C\) may be returned to \(\ket{0}_C\) by applying the
inverse computation to the retained correlated registers.  This is local
logical reset by uncomputation, not destruction of the global information.

\emph{No reset without information transfer.}
Let \(\ket{\phi_1}_B\) and \(\ket{\phi_2}_B\) be normalized states and suppose
a unitary on \(B\) and an environment \(E\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
 U_{BE}\ket{\phi_i}_B\ket{0}_E
 =
 \ket{0}_B\ket{e_i}_E,
 \qquad i=1,2 .
\label{eq:reset-transfer}
\end{equation}
Preservation of inner products gives
\begin{equation}
\braket{\phi_1|\phi_2}
 =
 \braket{e_1|e_2}.
\end{equation}
Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:reset-transfer} shows that resetting \(B\) preserves the
distinguishing information in \(E\).
If the environment were also required to finish in one fixed state,
\(\ket{e_1}=\ket{e_2}\), then the two inputs would have to coincide as rays.
An isometry can reset a subsystem by transferring information, but it cannot
merge distinct global alternatives.

Consequently, if \(\ket{g(x)}_B\) encodes a nonconstant classical function
in orthogonal basis states, no unitary acting on \(B\) alone resets it for
every \(x\).  The register can instead be handled by
(i) applying the inverse computation to the retained correlated registers;
(ii) transferring the information to an ancillary system or environment; or
(iii) measuring it and retaining a classical record.  In the latter two
cases, any receiving memory that is to be reused must itself eventually be
reset.

Bennett's reversible-computation analysis shows how intermediate garbage can
be uncomputed~\cite{bennett-73,bennett-82,Bennett2003}.  Thermodynamic
erasure is a separate physical question.  Resetting an unknown memory drawn
from a nontrivial ensemble for cyclic reuse generally requires entropy
export, but the minimum work or heat depends on the ensemble, bath, protocol,
and available side information~\cite{landauer:61,delrio2011thermodynamic}.
A known standard state does not incur a universal \(k_{\rm B}T\ln2\) cost
merely because it occupies one bit of hardware.  Whether the
Landauer--Bennett account fully resolves Maxwell-demon arguments remains a
philosophical dispute~\cite{Earman1998435,Earman19991,Norton2005}; the
operational statement used here is only that unitary dynamics preserves
information globally.

\section{Copyability is basis-dependent}

Classical information can be copied because it is encoded in distinguishable states. Quantum information cannot generally be copied.
If a unitary cloner existed with
\[
U\ket{\psi}\ket{0}=\ket{\psi}\ket{\psi}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
U\ket{\phi}\ket{0}=\ket{\phi}\ket{\phi},
\]
then unitarity preserves inner products: on the one side,
\[
\bra{\psi}\bra{0}\,U^{\dagger}U\,\ket{\phi}\ket{0}
= \braket{\psi|\phi}\,\braket{0|0}
= \braket{\psi|\phi},
\]
while on the other side the cloned outputs give
\[
\braket{\psi\,\psi\,|\,\phi\,\phi}
= \braket{\psi|\phi}^{2}.
\]
Equating the inner products and taking absolute values gives
\[
        \left|\braket{\psi|\phi}\right|
        =
        \left|\braket{\psi|\phi}\right|^2 .
\]
Hence distinct clonable pure states are orthogonal; the alternative
\(\left|\braket{\psi|\phi}\right|=1\) means that the normalized states
coincide as rays.  This is the no-cloning theorem~\cite{wo-zu,dieks}.  For
mixed states, the no-broadcasting theorem says that a set of density
operators can be broadcast if and only if they pairwise
commute~\cite{Barnum1996}.  The no-deletion theorem gives a related
obstruction: an unknown state cannot be deleted from one of two copies while
the other is left intact under the stated closed-system
conditions~\cite{PatiBraunstein2000}.

These results identify a precise boundary of copyability.  Within a specified
orthonormal basis one may copy basis labels,
\[
\ket{x}\ket{0}\mapsto\ket{x}\ket{x},
\]
but this does not clone an arbitrary superposition of those states.  More
generally, perfectly distinguishable classical labels can be redundantly
recorded, and broadcastable mixed-state families commute.

Here a \emph{context} means a maximal compatible set of observables,
equivalently a maximal Boolean subalgebra of the projection lattice.  In the
nondegenerate finite-dimensional case it corresponds to an orthonormal basis
and the projectors generated by it.  A merely compatible subset need not be
maximal.

Quantum computation can therefore manipulate information that is not
globally copyable.  A specified measurement instrument produces an outcome
record in a chosen readout algebra.  Environmental decoherence can select
stable pointer information and suppress interference, but by itself it
should not be identified with the production of one unique outcome in every
interpretation.

\section{Contextuality is not mere basis dependence}

Quantum algorithms use computational, phase, Fourier, and other bases, but a
change of representation is not by itself a physical effect, and applying a
Hadamard or Fourier transform is a unitary operation, not by itself a
demonstration of contextuality.  Kochen--Specker contextuality concerns a
stronger obstruction involving compatible measurement contexts.

In Hilbert spaces of dimension at least three, the Kochen--Specker theorem
rules out a global noncontextual value assignment satisfying the spectral and
functional constraints---for example, assigning exactly one value \(1\) to
every complete orthogonal resolution of the identity---independently of the
other compatible observables measured alongside it~\cite{kochen1,bell-66,
mermin-93,cabello2021contextuality}.  This does not rule out contextual
hidden-variable models.

For a single qubit, the standard Kochen--Specker obstruction for projective
measurements is absent.
Broader operational
notions extend contextuality to preparations, transformations, and unsharp
measurements~\cite{Spekkens-04}.  The present partition formalism does not
depend on resolving that terminological dispute.

Each partition observable \(A_\Pi\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:partition-observable} is an ordinary Hermitian operator.
Different partition observables may fail to commute, so their labels need not
be jointly accessible on one system.  That complementarity constrains
complete identification, but it becomes a Kochen--Specker claim only when a
specified collection of contexts satisfies the theorem's global
nonassignment conditions.

This separates two questions.  Partition-resolving recombination explains
how the accessible answer is produced; a resource theory asks why the same
input--output statistics resist efficient classical simulation.  Stabilizer
operations admit efficient classical simulation under the
Gottesman--Knill assumptions~\cite{GottesmanKnill,AaronsonGottesman2004};
universal quantum computation in the stabilizer/magic-state model therefore
requires a resource outside that fragment.  Depending on the model and
dimension, nonstabilizer resources may be described by magic,
quasiprobability negativity, or contextuality~\cite{Howard-2014,Veitch2014,
Raussendorf2013,Raussendorf2020PhaseSpace}.  The cleanest equivalences occur
in specified odd-prime-dimensional qudit settings, while qubits require
additional qualifications.  Contextuality is therefore neither asserted nor
needed here as a necessary-and-sufficient explanation of every quantum
speedup.

\section{Quantum universality versus Turing universality}

The word ``universal'' has related but distinct uses in quantum
information and in the theory of computation.  A finite local gate set,
together with the ability to apply its gates to specified qubits subject to
the architecture, is approximately universal when, for every register size
\(n\), every target unitary \(U\) on that register, and every accuracy
\(\epsilon>0\), a finite circuit over the gate set approximates \(U\) up to
the physically irrelevant global phase.  This quantifies over all \(n\);
for a fixed \(n\), the resulting circuits densely generate the appropriate
unitary group.  Density is an approximation statement.  Compilation cost
also depends on how the target is specified, and a generic \(n\)-qubit
unitary has an exponentially large description~\cite{DawsonNielsen2006}.

Turing universality concerns effective symbolic procedures on finite inputs
of unbounded length.  A Turing-universal model can simulate any effective
procedure under a finite encoding~\cite{turing-36,turing-37-correction}.
This is the setting for partial recursive functions, undecidability,
unbounded work space and time, and the halting problem.

A single circuit on \(n\) qubits implements one finite-dimensional map
\[
        U_n:\mathbb C^{2^n}\longrightarrow\mathbb C^{2^n}.
\]
It has fixed width and depth.  A circuit family
\(\{C_n\}_{n\geq1}\) may nevertheless define a language even when it is
nonuniform; such a family can hide arbitrary advice in the choice of its
circuits.  It constitutes an effective algorithmic model when a uniform rule
generates \(C_n\) from \(n\).  For complexity-theoretic claims this rule is
normally required to obey an explicit resource bound, such as polynomial
time or logarithmic space.

With standard uniformity and error conventions, uniform quantum circuit
families and quantum Turing machines yield equivalent bounded-error or
Monte Carlo complexity classes.  Exact and Las Vegas formulations require
additional qualifications~\cite{deutsch,be-va,nishimura-ozawa-2002}.
Thus the two models are positively related by simulation theorems, but that
equivalence is not supplied merely by the existence of a universal gate set.

For finite classical outputs, an effective halting condition, computable gate
or transition amplitudes, and a fixed bounded-error gap, standard quantum
models do not decide nonrecursive languages.  Their possible advantage is
one of complexity, not an enlargement of classical computability.  Allowing
noncomputable parameters or nonuniform noncomputable advice would change the
model by inserting the desired information into its specification.

Finally, a Turing machine need not be modeled as repeatedly entering one
many-to-one absorbing state: its transition function may simply stop being
applied.  The narrower unitary observation remains valid.  If
\(U\ket{h}=\ket{h}\) and \(U\ket{\psi}=\ket{h}\), injectivity implies
\(\ket{\psi}=\ket{h}\).  Closed unitary dynamics therefore cannot merge
distinct histories into one absorbing state, but standard quantum models
implement operational termination with halt flags, clocks, external
control, or measurement protocols~\cite{deutsch,be-va,myers-1997,
ozawa-1998}.  There is no conflict with quantum Turing universality.

\section{Conclusion}

Quantum computation does not make the values associated with superposition
components independently readable.  It can instead encode a problem into a
partition of its instance set and use coherent dynamics to map the cells of
that partition to orthogonal output subspaces.  The resulting answer can be
resolved by the spectral projectors of a Hermitian observable or,
equivalently, by a unitary that writes the cell label to a standard readout
register.

This formulation also preserves the possibility of complete identification.
A separating family of partitions has the singleton partition as its common
refinement.  If the corresponding observables can be jointly or sequentially
implemented with the required resources, their labels identify the complete
instance.  Alternatively, a single enlarged unitary may resolve the refined
label directly.  Quantum algorithms need not be restricted to permanently
coarse answers; the issue is whether the required partition or refinement
admits an efficient physical realization.  Parity makes this caveat vivid:
even a two-cell partition can be hard to resolve when its cells are globally
interlaced and the oracle exposes their label only through a high-degree
dependence on all input coordinates.

The Bernstein--Vazirani leakage model makes the distinction between
evaluation and resolution explicit.  The answer function is evaluated by a
globally unitary interaction even when a witness records path information.
After the witness becomes inaccessible, the reduced channel loses selected
coherences and therefore loses the corresponding partition-resolving
interference.  Orthogonal witness records produce complete dephasing of the
selected coordinates, while nonorthogonal records give a continuous
visibility parameter.  What is lost is accessible recombination, not global
reversibility.

The broader constraints must remain separate.  A subsystem can be reset
unitarily only by retaining its distinguishing information elsewhere;
thermodynamic erasure concerns the later physical reset of that information.
Unknown states are not generally clonable.  Basis changes are not by
themselves Kochen--Specker contextuality, although specified collections of
partition observables can instantiate contextuality scenarios.  Finally,
universal gate sets and Turing-universal uniform models are connected by
simulation results but are not identical definitions.

Partition-resolving interference is therefore an operational framework, not
a universal sufficient condition for speedup.  Sampling tasks, quantum-state
outputs, and other computational models may require a different immediate
description.  Within the oracle and identification problems considered here,
however, the framework states the central point precisely: useful information
is engineered into resolvable spectral alternatives, not recovered by
branchwise inspection.

\section{A unitary leakage knob for Bernstein--Vazirani interference}
\label{app:leaky-bv}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The Bernstein--Vazirani algorithm gives a particularly transparent
example of quantum speedup by interference rather than by any literal
parallel readout of classical function values~\cite{be-va,
nielsen-book,NaseriEtAl2022}.  Let
\(a\in\mathbb F_2^n\) be an unknown string and define
\begin{equation}
        f_a(x)=a\cdot x \pmod 2 ,
        \qquad x\in\mathbb F_2^n .
\end{equation}
The usual reversible oracle is
\begin{equation}
        O_a\ket{x}\ket{y}
        =
        \ket{x}\ket{y\oplus f_a(x)} .
\end{equation}
With the target register initialized in
\(\ket{-}=(\ket{0}-\ket{1})/\sqrt 2\), this oracle acts by phase
kickback,
\[
        O_a\ket{x}\ket{-}
        =
        (-1)^{a\cdot x}\ket{x}\ket{-}.
\]
Thus
\begin{align}
\ket{0^n}\ket{-}
&\xrightarrow{H^{\otimes n}}
2^{-n/2}\sum_x \ket{x}\ket{-}  \nonumber \\
&\xrightarrow{O_a}
2^{-n/2}\sum_x (-1)^{a\cdot x}\ket{x}\ket{-}
 \nonumber \\
&\xrightarrow{H^{\otimes n}}
\ket{a}\ket{-}.
\end{align}
Equivalently, the final amplitude of \(\ket{z}\) is
\begin{equation}
        2^{-n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_2^n}
        (-1)^{(a\oplus z)\cdot x}
        =
        \delta_{z,a}.
\end{equation}
The hidden string is therefore recovered exactly with one coherent query.
The essential step is not the evaluation of many classical values, but
the conversion of a global phase character into a computational-basis
population by a Fourier transform over \(\mathbb F_2^n\).

We now introduce a reversible modification that degrades this
interference without changing the Boolean function \(f_a\).  Let
\(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) have cardinality \(k\), and let
\(\bar S\) denote its complement.  For \(x\in\mathbb F_2^n\), write
\[
        x_S=(x_i)_{i\in S}\in\mathbb F_2^k,
        \qquad
        x_{\bar S}=(x_i)_{i\in \bar S}\in\mathbb F_2^{n-k},
\]
where a fixed ordering of the coordinates in \(S\) and \(\bar S\) is
understood.  Thus \(x_S\) and \(x_{\bar S}\) denote the subvectors of
\(x\) supported on \(S\) and on its complement, respectively.  Define the
unitary permutation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-bv-oracle}
        W_a^S\ket{x}\ket{y}\ket{e}
        =
        \ket{x}\ket{y\oplus f_a(x)}\ket{e\oplus x_S},
\end{equation}
where the third register is a witness, garbage, or environmental
register.  The oracle still computes the same function \(f_a\), and the
enlarged map is reversible.  However, if the third register is not
coherently available to the algorithm, then it carries which-path
information about the computational-basis label \(x\).  Here
``which-path'' is meant in the standard interferometric sense: a record
relative to a chosen computational basis, not an independently readable
classical computation carried by a component of the superposition.  In
this respect the construction is a tunable version of the familiar
which-way suppression of interference~\cite{PhysRevA.25.2208,Englert1996,
RevModPhys.75.715,Lent2020}.  It is also closely related to Aaronson's
Problem~11 on standard versus erasing oracles with input-dependent garbage,
where sufficiently informative garbage is suggested to behave, under
appropriate conditions, like decoherence or measurement of the
response~\cite{Aaronson2021OpenProblems}.

To specify the accessible query model, each call below receives a fresh
witness register in \(\ket{0^k}\), and that register is discarded after
the interaction.  Thus the algorithm has access to the memoryless channel
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-bv-accessible-channel}
 \Phi_a^S(\rho)
 =\operatorname{Tr}_{E}\!\left[
 W_a^S\bigl(\rho\otimes\ket{0^k}\!\bra{0^k}_{E}\bigr)
 (W_a^S)^\dagger\right]
\end{equation}
on the query and answer registers, not to the inverse of the enlarged
unitary.  This distinction fixes the oracle interface for repeated calls.
For a computational-basis value query \(\ket{x}\ket{0}\), discarding the
witness does not obscure the definite answer \(f_a(x)\); it suppresses
only coherences between inputs carrying distinguishable witness records.
Accordingly, this is structured coordinate dephasing, not generic
depolarizing noise: it preserves the within-block coherences that resolve
\(a_{\bar S}\), completely removes selected cross-block coherences when
the records are orthogonal, and leaves ordinary basis-state value queries
intact.

For later query-complexity statements, the phase-kickback restriction of
the accessible channel is useful to record explicitly:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-bv-reduced-channel}
        \mathcal E_a^S(\rho)
        =
        D_a
        \left(
        \sum_{s\in\mathbb F_2^k} P_s \rho P_s
        \right)
        D_a^\dagger ,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
        D_a=\sum_x (-1)^{a\cdot x}\ket{x}\bra{x},
        \qquad
        P_s=\sum_{x:x_S=s}\ket{x}\bra{x} .
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in matrix elements,
\begin{equation}
        \mathcal E_a^S(\rho)
        =
        \sum_{\substack{x,x'\\x_S=x'_S}}
        (-1)^{a\cdot(x\oplus x')}
        \rho_{x x'}\ket{x}\bra{x'} .
\end{equation}
This is the phase-interference restriction, not the full accessible
query--answer channel \(\Phi_a^S\): the latter still depends on \(a_S\)
through the answer-register flip, which is why ordinary value queries can
recover the hidden components.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Proposition: partial loss of the Bernstein--Vazirani interference pattern.}
Let the query register be initialized in \(\ket{+}^{\otimes n}\), the
answer register in \(\ket{-}\), and the witness register in
\(\ket{0^k}\).  Apply \(W_a^S\), trace out the witness register, and
then apply \(H^{\otimes n}\) to the query register.  The final
measurement distribution on the query register is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-bv-distribution}
        \Pr(z)
        =
        2^{-k}\,
        \mathbf 1\!\left[z_{\bar S}=a_{\bar S}\right] .
\end{equation}
Thus one coherent Bernstein--Vazirani-style query determines the
\(n-k\) components of \(a\) outside \(S\) with certainty, while the
observed coordinates \(z_S\) are uniformly distributed over
\(\mathbb F_2^k\) and carry no information about \(a_S\).  In
particular, the usual one-query speedup survives only on those
coordinates whose computational-basis path labels have not been exported
to the inaccessible witness.

In the language of Sec.~\ref{sec:partition-operators}, this measurement
exactly resolves the partition
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_S=\{C_u:u\in\mathbb F_2^{n-k}\},\qquad
 C_u=\{a\in\mathbb F_2^n:a_{\bar S}=u\}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, after a fixed reordering of output coordinates, the Hermitian
observable
\begin{equation}
 A_{\Pi_S}=\sum_{u\in\mathbb F_2^{n-k}}\lambda_u
 \bigl(\ket{u}\!\bra{u}_{\bar S}\otimes I_S\bigr),
 \qquad \lambda_u\ne\lambda_v\ \text{for }u\ne v,
\end{equation}
has spectral projectors labelled by the cells of \(\Pi_S\).  The remaining
value queries refine this partition to the singleton partition and hence
complete the identification of \(a\).

Operationally, after this first query one may recover the remaining
coordinates by \(k\) ordinary value queries on the standard basis vectors
\(e_i\), \(i\in S\), since \(f_a(e_i)=a_i\).  This provides an explicit completion strategy with query count \(1+k\) for \(k<n\).  At the endpoint \(k=n\), the first
Bernstein--Vazirani-style query gives the uniform distribution and is
best omitted, so the standard \(n\) value queries suffice.  Thus the
interference step and ordinary value recovery are not opposed: a
structure-matched interference readout can supply the part of the answer
that remains coherently recombinable, while additional value queries can
complete the full classical string.  Hence this simple strategy
interpolates as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-bv-query-cost}
        Q_{\rm strat}(n,k)
        =
        \begin{cases}
        1+k, & 0\leq k<n,\\
        n,   & k=n.
        \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:leaky-bv-query-cost} describes an explicit completion
strategy, not a general optimality theorem for arbitrary adaptive use of
the full leaked channel.  The structural fact proved below is narrower:
the accessible phase-interference channel contains no dependence on
\(a_S\).  Once \(a_{\bar S}\) is known, the \(k\) standard-basis value
queries used here provide \(k\) independent linear equations and are
necessary within this restricted second-stage strategy.  Optimal query
complexity for arbitrary adaptive protocols using the full
query--answer channel is a separate problem and is not claimed here.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Proof.}
After the first Hadamard transform and the application of \(W_a^S\), the
joint state is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-bv-joint-state}
        \ket{\psi_a^S}
        =
        2^{-n/2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_2^n}
        (-1)^{a\cdot x}\ket{x}\ket{-}\ket{x_S}.
\end{equation}
Tracing out the witness register gives the reduced density operator on
the query register
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-bv-reduced-state}
        \rho_a^S
        =
        2^{-n}
        \sum_{\substack{x,x'\in\mathbb F_2^n\\ x_S=x'_S}}
        (-1)^{a\cdot(x\oplus x')}
        \ket{x}\bra{x'} .
\end{equation}
Hence coherences remain only between computational-basis components that
agree on the coordinates in \(S\).  The witness register has therefore
implemented a perfect which-path record for those coordinates.

Now apply \(H^{\otimes n}\) and measure in the computational basis.  For
\(z\in\mathbb F_2^n\),
\begin{align}
        \Pr(z)
        &=
        \bra{z}H^{\otimes n}\rho_a^S H^{\otimes n}\ket{z}       \\
        &=
        2^{-2n}
        \sum_{\substack{x,x'\in\mathbb F_2^n\\ x_S=x'_S}}
        (-1)^{a\cdot(x\oplus x')+z\cdot(x\oplus x')} .
\end{align}
Set \(t=x\oplus x'\).  The constraint \(x_S=x'_S\) is equivalent to
\(t_S=0\).  For each such \(t\), there are \(2^n\) choices of \(x\).
Therefore
\begin{align}
        \Pr(z)
        &=
        2^{-n}
        \sum_{\substack{t\in\mathbb F_2^n\\ t_S=0}}
        (-1)^{(a\oplus z)\cdot t}                                      \\
        &=
        2^{-n}
        \sum_{t_{\bar S}\in\mathbb F_2^{n-k}}
        (-1)^{(a_{\bar S}\oplus z_{\bar S})\cdot t_{\bar S}} .
\end{align}
By character orthogonality over \(\mathbb F_2^{n-k}\),
\begin{equation}
        \sum_{t_{\bar S}\in\mathbb F_2^{n-k}}
        (-1)^{(a_{\bar S}\oplus z_{\bar S})\cdot t_{\bar S}}
        =
        \begin{cases}
        2^{n-k}, & z_{\bar S}=a_{\bar S},\\
        0,       & z_{\bar S}\neq a_{\bar S}.
        \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:leaky-bv-distribution}.

The block form above identifies what information remains available through
the phase-interference channel.  Inside each block \(x_S=x'_S=s\), the
contribution of \(a_S\) cancels:
\begin{equation}
        a\cdot(x\oplus x')
        =
        a_{\bar S}\cdot(x_{\bar S}\oplus x'_{\bar S}) .
\end{equation}
Therefore the reduced phase-interference channel depends on \(a\) only
through \(a_{\bar S}\).  The hidden components \(a_S\) are not merely
hard to extract by the final Hadamard transform; they are absent from
the accessible phase channel once the witness has been traced out.

The same dephasing in the \(x_S\) basis occurs for any preparation of the answer register, since the witness records \(x_S\) independently of the answer-register state. The dephasing removes the dependence on $a_S$ from the accessible \emph{phase-interference} channel; it does not remove ordinary value information carried by the answer register. Consequently, once $a_{\bar S}$ has been obtained by the surviving Bernstein--Vazirani interference, the remaining components $a_S$ can be recovered by standard value-query use of
\begin{equation}
        f_a(x)=a_S\cdot x_S+a_{\bar S}\cdot x_{\bar S}.
\end{equation}
Since $a_{\bar S}$ is then known, a standard value query supplies one linear equation over the unknown vector $a_S\in\mathbb F_2^k$. Querying the standard basis vectors $e_i$, $i\in S$, recovers these $k$ components directly. This proves the stated interpolation strategy in Eq.~\eqref{eq:leaky-bv-query-cost}. \(\square\)

\subsection{Continuous witness overlap}

The preceding construction has a literal continuous version.  For every
\(i\in S\), choose normalized witness states
\(\ket{\epsilon_0^{(i)}}\) and \(\ket{\epsilon_1^{(i)}}\) with real overlap
\begin{equation}
 \braket{\epsilon_0^{(i)}|\epsilon_1^{(i)}}=\lambda_i,
 \qquad 0\leq\lambda_i\leq 1,
\end{equation}
and put
\(\ket{\epsilon_{x_S}}=\bigotimes_{i\in S}
\ket{\epsilon_{x_i}^{(i)}}\).  A controlled isometry, extendable to a
unitary on a sufficiently large witness space, can act on its initialized
subspace as
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde W_{a}^{S,\boldsymbol\lambda}
 \ket{x}\ket{y}\ket{0}_E
 =\ket{x}\ket{y\oplus f_a(x)}\ket{\epsilon_{x_S}}.
\end{equation}
The retained query label \(x\) makes the images of distinct computational
basis inputs orthogonal, so nonorthogonality of the witness states does not
conflict with this unitary extension.

After phase kickback and tracing out the witness, the query-register matrix
elements are
\begin{equation}
 \rho_{x x'}=2^{-n}(-1)^{a\cdot(x\oplus x')}
 \prod_{i\in S}\lambda_i^{x_i\oplus x_i'}.
\end{equation}
The final Hadamard transform therefore gives the explicit distribution
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:continuous-leaky-bv-distribution}
 \Pr\nolimits_{\boldsymbol\lambda}(z)
 =\mathbf 1[z_{\bar S}=a_{\bar S}]
 \prod_{i\in S}
 \frac{1+(-1)^{z_i\oplus a_i}\lambda_i}{2}.
\end{equation}
One way to see the factorization is to note that each leaked coordinate
has a reduced two-dimensional state whose off-diagonal coherence is
multiplied by \(\lambda_i\); the last Hadamard then returns the correct bit
with probability \((1+\lambda_i)/2\).  In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \Pr\nolimits_{\boldsymbol\lambda}(z=a)
 =\prod_{i\in S}\frac{1+\lambda_i}{2}.
\end{equation}
The endpoint \(\lambda_i=1\) leaves that coordinate fully coherent, whereas
\(\lambda_i=0\) makes the two witness records orthogonal and yields the
uniform factor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:leaky-bv-distribution}.  Intermediate
overlaps thus continuously interpolate the visibility of the corresponding
partition-resolving interference.  Establishing an optimal adaptive query
complexity for every overlap vector is a separate problem; the formula
above characterizes the stated one-query readout exactly.
If \(\lambda_i=1\) for only some \(i\in S\), those coordinates are
effectively not leaked and the corresponding factors reduce to
\(\mathbf 1[z_i=a_i]\); the resolved partition is then finer than the
partition associated with \(S\) alone.

\medskip
The two extremes make the physical point explicit.  For \(k=0\), no
computational-basis path label is exported and the ordinary one-query
Bernstein--Vazirani interference pattern is recovered.  For \(k=n\), the
witness records the entire computational-basis label \(x\), all
off-diagonal terms in that basis are removed from the algorithm's
accessible state, and the final Hadamard transform has no global phase
pattern left to recombine.  The Boolean function \(f_a\) has still been
evaluated coherently by a unitary permutation on the enlarged system.
What has been lost is not evaluation but recombination.

This example shows that reversibility alone is not the relevant
criterion.  The map \(W_a^S\) is a unitary permutation on the enlarged
Hilbert space.  If the witness register remains coherently accessible,
one may uncompute it and restore the full interference pattern.  If,
however, the witness register has escaped into an inaccessible
environment, then the reduced dynamics available to the algorithm is no
longer reversible.  The witness register is precisely the residual
garbage identified in the main text as an obstruction to free unitary
reset, here promoted to a tunable knob.  The one-query Bernstein--Vazirani
readout advantage is lost in this leakage model not because the Boolean
function \(f_a\) has changed, nor because the global dynamics has ceased to
be unitary, but because the inaccessible witness dephases the coherences
needed for the later Fourier-basis recombination.

Pointedly stated, this demonstrates that a quantum computer does not
``compute all branches at once and let us independently read them''---where the
scare-quoted branch language names precisely the classical picture being
rejected.  If it did,
leaking the computational-basis path labels to a witness register would
not matter: the function \(f_a(x)\) was still computed.  Instead, the
appendix shows that the speedup relies on \emph{recombining amplitudes}
by interference in the basis matched to the hidden linear character.
When uncomputation is blocked by an inaccessible witness, the accessible
dynamics is dephased in the computational basis, the interference is
quenched, and the lost coordinates must be recovered, if at all, by
ordinary value queries.
